# General practice care for people with a mental health condition

### Key findings

This report summarises the second proof-of-concept general practice data development project undertaken in partnership between the WA Primary Health Alliance and the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare. The project explored de-identified unit record (patient-level) general practice data to understand its potential to provide health insights for people experiencing a mental health condition. The report highlights these insights as well as provides recommendations on data sharing, governance, technical and analytical platforms, and data quality to guide future efforts towards developing a national primary health care data collection.

# General practice care for people with a mental health condition

## Summary

### Project description

The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) and the WA Primary Health Alliance (WAPHA) partnered to explore health outcomes of people with mental health conditions recorded in general practice. We used seven years (1 July 2017 to 30 June 2024) of de-identified unit record Western Australian (WA) general practice patient data.

The project is one of several general practice data demonstration projects that will help us to build a future National Primary Health Care Data Collection (NPHCDC).

#### Project objectives

Separate aspects of this project were taken alongside these objectives to explore the potential of general practice to provide prevalence and expenditure information for conditions that may be diagnosed and managed in primary care.

- Describe general practice activity and physical health outcomes among general practice patients with a recorded mental health condition in WA.
- Assess the quality and characteristics of the shared WAPHA general practice data, including identifying strengths, limitations and opportunities for improvement.
- Generate insights to inform the development of an NPHCDC, including assessing the value of unit record general practice data for population health monitoring, research, policy and planning.

### Key findings

The project provided valuable evidence on the physical health burden, primary care use and the role of general practice for people living with mental health conditions in WA.

- Up to 4 in 10 people in WA with a recorded mental health condition live with co-existing physical health conditions.
- Up to 9 in 10 people in WA with a recorded mental health condition have one or more recorded biomedical risk factors or health behaviours.
- People with mental health conditions in WA attend general practice more frequently than people without these conditions.
- People with mental health conditions in WA receive a higher rate of chronic condition management through general practice compared to people without these conditions.
- General practice data can help address critical information gaps in mental health service delivery and population health reporting.

### Project insights

The project provided valuable evidence on the partnerships, governance, technical systems and capability, and data quality considerations needed for the NPHCDC. The key insights below highlight the project's most significant lessons and areas for future development.

- Secure sharing of de-identified unit record data is feasible through governance and custodianship arrangements piloted in this project.
- The Primary Health Insights (PHI) platform enabled secure collaboration and supported efficient sharing, storage and analysis of data.
- General practice patient data can provide valuable health insights and help address key information gaps.
- Strong ongoing sector collaboration will be critical to optimise the usefulness of general practice data and ensure the success of an NPHCDC.

### Next steps

This project demonstrated the value of de-identified general practice patient data for addressing important health information gaps. Primary Health Networks (PHNs) are crucial partners with the AIHW in establishing a national collection that serves the needs of the primary care sector. In that partnership, we've identified the following key next steps:

- Strengthen novel governance arrangements explored through the project. AIHW will continue to engage the sector to test and implement a comprehensive National Primary Health Care Data Governance Framework to support the appropriate, safe and secure collection, management and use of general practice data for the purposes of an NPHCDC.
- Continue to explore PHI for a national primary health care data collection.
- Maintain and strengthen relationships with all stakeholders to make the best use of the data in its current form while pursuing ongoing improvements to optimise its value.
- Build on project findings to further highlight the role of general practice data to provide care and health insights. In partnership with our stakeholders, AIHW will expand future work to other health conditions.
- Work to understand First Nations identification in general practice data and partnering with First Nations health organisations to explore First Nations primary care data. AIHW is establishing a First Nations Leadership Group to advise the development of an NPHCDC that is culturally appropriate, safe and strength based.

The project provides key insights on the health status and general practice care received by people who live with a mental health condition in Western Australia (WA). This highlights general practice data’s potential to provide insights into service delivery and health outcomes for a priority area for Australia’s health system.

#### Study description

We undertook a cross-sectional study for the 12-month period of the 2023–24 financial year. All outcomes are provided for the 2023–24 financial year, for more information about study design and methodology, including outcomes and their measurement see General practice care for people with mental health conditions in Western Australia methods.

All findings are descriptive in nature as no weighting or consideration of causal assumptions were undertaken due to project scope and time limitations.

The study aimed to describe:

The study was limited to people aged 18 years and over.

Project data was WA Primary Health Alliance (WAPHA) Primary Sense data using the International Classification of Primary Care, second edition (ICPC-2), Australian extension (ICPC-2 PLUS) to create cohorts from diagnostic data. A subset of seven years of WAPHA Primary Sense data was available from 1 July 2017 to 30 June 2024, so it was possible to count recorded diagnoses historically to create cohorts. For more information about study data, see technical notes.

- demographics (age, gender, geographic breakdown)
- rates and types of comorbidities
- rates and types of biomedical risk factors and health behaviours
- general practice care (chronic condition management and multidisciplinary care proxies)
- general practice attendance patterns for each cohort.

The project was approved by the AIHW Ethics Committee using the existing data governance arrangements between Primary Health Networks (PHNs) and general practices. WAPHA ensured all participating practices, including clinicians and practice staff, were provided with information about the study and given the opportunity to opt-out of data sharing with AIHW for this project.

The sharing of de-identified general practice data sharing is managed securely by Australian general practices, as part of regular management of patient (consumer) consent for data sharing. For purposes other than those directly related to patient care, general practices are legally bound under the Commonwealth Privacy Act 1988 to ensure no information that could identify a patient leaves the practice environment.

The study did not disaggregate results of First Nations people using general practices. At the time the project was established, there were concerns about the quality and completeness of Indigenous identification in the source data and it was considered unlikely that the data would be suitable for reporting by Indigenous status. For example, among the 6,244 general practices included in the aggregate Practice Incentives Program Quality Improvement (PIPQI) Eligible Data Set, 20% of regular clients aged 15 years and over had a Not Stated Indigenous status in their GP record. Data on how this varies by PHN is available at *Practice Incentives Program Quality Improvement Measures: annual data update 2025–26, First Nations people data*. Project data did not include data from the Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations (ACCHO) sector.

This represents a missed opportunity, particularly as evidence suggests that more than half of all First Nations people receive their care from mainstream general practices (AIHW 2024, AIHW 2025). Lessons from this project have informed subsequent primary health care data initiatives, including the collection of Indigenous status information to support assessment of data quality, transparency about data limitations and future reporting where appropriate.

Future data demonstration projects will explore identification of First Nations people in general practice data, in consultation with the First Nations health consumers, health professionals and organisations.

The AIHW is partnering with sector stakeholders to ensure appropriate Indigenous Data Governance arrangements are in place for the development of a National Primary Health Care Data Collection (NPHCDC). AIHW remains committed to ensuring First Nations people are represented in an NPHCDC, and that data about First Nations people using mainstream general practices can lead to meaningful improvements in health outcomes for First Nations people. AIHW is also exploring the requirements to make the collection suitable for inclusion of data from Aboriginal Medical Services (including ACCHOs). For more information, see Next steps.

- Data collection methods. Study data is limited to conditions recorded in general practice clinical records and the presence or absence of a recorded diagnostic code. Other available estimates are self-reported and rely on statistical methods to weight to the population level.

Certain conditions may be managed more frequently in general practice due to a combination of clinical presentation and traditional reasons. For example, people with hypertension may attend general practice more often for maintenance and management than people who use illicit drugs.
- Cultural and social factors, such as stigma associated with a condition. These factors may make it more or less likely for individuals to disclose symptoms and/or other aspects like health behaviours. These cultural and social factors may also impact how easily, or frequently general practice staff ask about symptoms and/or health behaviours.
- Data input practices. General practice staff may have individual or variable practices of recording diagnoses.

#### Why is using general practice data for expenditure estimation important?

- Accurately understanding what happens within a general practice consult is key for developing robust national disease expenditure estimates for general practice services.
- Current methods for allocating general practice and Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) expenditure to disease and injuries relies on outdated survey data to allocate costs to specific conditions. For example, existing methods utilise the outdated Bettering the Evaluation of Care of Health (BEACH) data, which has significant limitations due to the continued release of new items onto the PBS, changing utilisation of pharmaceuticals and changing service trends within general practice.
- Due to the generic nature of many pharmaceuticals, which can be prescribed for different diseases and injuries, allocation of expenditure requires an analysis of diagnostic and prescription patterns to be able to accurately allocate expenditure to conditions.

### Understanding the utility of general practice data for disease expenditure reporting

From exploration and analysis of project data, we found general practice data to be highly useful to understand general practice service utilisation in relation to ABDS conditions within Western Australia (WA), and potentially transferable for national reporting of disease estimates.

For this exploratory work, the AIHW comprehensively mapped ABDS conditions to diagnostic codes contained in project data (ICPC-2 PLUS codes and sub-descriptions). We created weighted counts of services by ABDS condition, age group, gender and financial year between 2018–19 and 2023–24. Current work is ongoing to compare trends and service distributions to existing data to verify service weight tables derived from WA general practice data.

In addition to exploring and developing refined estimates for general practice expenditure, work is currently ongoing to compare diagnostic information to prescription data.

#### Data quality is generally high and supported study population-level analyses

Overall, the project demonstrated that WAPHA's general practice data set is of sufficient quality to support study objects. The project identified general practice data to be valuable for other use cases, such as population health monitoring, service planning and applied research.

- Completeness of data tables is generally high.
- The instances of errors such as mismatches in unique identifiers is very rare, impacting <1% of patient records.

#### Representativeness of the data set

The study included data from 430 general practices across Western Australia (WA) providing a view of primary care from one large Australian state. This also represented a large sample of general practice data, with a structure of data for around half of all PHNs – those using the Primary Sense extractor.

#### Areas for improvement

There were some rare instances of data missingness that match WAPHA’s own assessments or were already known to AIHW from PIPQI data submissions:

There is low completeness in recording living status (lives alone or resides in Residential Aged Care Facility). Given the important of living status to health outcomes, improving the completeness of these fields represents an opportunity for the sector to improve health outcomes.

- 70% completeness in the Reason For Encounter Field (RFE, Visit Reason in Primary Sense data). In this project, diagnostic information is drawn from both RFE and the Past History (or Clinical History) fields. Primary Sense ensures full de-identification of RFE fields before the data leaves the practice and this field was found to be a useful source of diagnostic information. However, further work is required to validate when the RFE field encodes for a clinical visit, and can be counted as diagnostic information, or a different kind of visit, such as for obtaining a referral or prescription. The AIHW continues to explore and enhance the quality and usefulness of the RFE field in ongoing general practice data demonstration projects. For more information, see Data demonstration projects.
- Only 70–80% completeness of recording of certain risk factors (smoking, alcohol consumption). This finding represents a low level of missingness, and an opportunity for improvement for the sector.

### Recording gender in general practice data

Gender reported in this report reflects the gender recorded by clinical staff during individual clinical encounters or entered by the patient when attending the practice. While this may have input from each patient, these recordings may not necessarily reflect patient’s identification with a particular gender. Response options for gender recording include, 'male', 'female', and 'other'. There were a very small number of patients whose gender was recorded as 'other', but these numbers were unable to be reported separately in order to preserve privacy and confidentiality of the patients. Patients whose gender is recorded as 'other' were still included in the study.

#### Rationale for counting rules used

All counting rule decisions were guided by AIHW and WA Primary Health Alliance (WAPHA) based subject matter experts. We adopted study counting rules for the following reasons:

Given the study design (further explained below) is cross-sectional and does not include any time series analyses, we consider these counting rules appropriate. Further work to validate counting rules would be worthwhile for the sector, however this work was not within scope of the current project.

- For anxiety, depression, and/or phobia/compulsive disorders (high prevalence mental health conditions): These conditions were considered episodic, due to relatively high rates of remission (Johnco et al. 2026). As such, we counted people with these conditions in the study reference year (2023–24). We acknowledge that remission does not occur for many people with these conditions.
- For schizophrenia and/or affective psychosis (low prevalence mental health conditions): We counted people with these conditions if a record occurred at any point in study data. This was to capture more people with these conditions (for more information on the limitation of study look-back period, see study time period issues).

#### Physical health conditions

Evaluation of comorbid physical health conditions does not account for all chronic health conditions. The study limited these analyses to highly prevalent conditions commonly managed in primary care.

Comorbid physical health conditions describe whether a diagnosis of a selected health conditions is present in a patient’s general practice record. Unique patient records are counted if a relevant International Classification of Primary Care, second edition (ICPC-2), Australian extension (ICPC-2 PLUS) diagnostic code appears in or before 2023–24. Where possible definitions for chronic health conditions were aligned with national standards. However, sometimes due to differences in data classifications and mapping, it was not always possible to exactly align definitions.

We also counted the number of times each of the physical health conditions above occurred for each unique patient record to describe multimorbidity.

- Chronic lower respiratory disease (CLRD): We counted someone as having CLRD if their record included one or more ICPC-2 PLUS codes for asthma, chronic bronchitis, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (including emphysema, and chronic airways limitation). This aligned with the AIHW definition nationally reported on asthma and COPD; for more information see Chronic respiratory conditions in Australia.
- Cardiovascular disease (CVD): We counted someone as having this condition if their record included one or more ICPC-2 PLUS codes for coronary heart disease, stroke, transient cerebral ischemia, heart failure and myopathy, atrial fibrillation, peripheral arterial disease, and rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease. There are differences in definitions between the study’s definition of CVD and other nationally reported definitions of heart, stroke, and vascular disease (HSVD). For example, the study estimate includes Transient Ischaemic Attack (TIA), whereas AIHW estimates and the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) National Health Survey (NHS) do not include TIA. There may also be other differences, stemming from the differences in data classification systems between the study data and data used for other estimates. The study uses data classified through ICPC-2 PLUS and other estimates use International Classification of Disease 10th edition Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM). For more information see Heart, stroke and vascular disease: Australian facts, Data sources. Further work to map definitions between estimates for CVD and HSVD using ICPC-2 PLUS and ICD-10-AM is warranted.
- Type 2 diabetes: We defined this condition to align with the AIHW definition nationally reported type 2 diabetes, for more information see What is diabetes? We counted someone as having this condition if their record contained ICPC-2 PLUS codes for type 2 diabetes. The majority of these codes are assigned to a patient record when a patient has been given a formal diagnosis of diabetes. Therefore, this estimate does not accurately capture the prevalence of people who have suspected diabetes and are awaiting further tests. AIHW is working with stakeholders to address known diabetes data issues and gaps. For more information, see Limitations of estimating diabetes prevalence and Data gaps and opportunities.
- Cancer: We counted someone as having cancer if their record contained ICPC-2 PLUS codes for all available types and bodily region of malignancy. However, there are many different types of cancer and useful comparisons between this study’s estimates and national estimates could only be meaningful if broken down by cancer type. For more information, see AIHW’s extensive reporting on cancer in Cancer data in Australia.
- Chronic kidney disease (CKD): We counted someone as having CKD if their record contained ICPC-2 PLUS codes for chronic kidney disease (all stages) and chronic renal failure. For more information on chronic kidney disease, see Chronic kidney disease.
- Chronic liver disease (CLD): We counted someone as having chronic liver disease if their record contained ICPC-2 PLUS codes for chronic liver disease and viral hepatitis.

#### Care measures

Care measures are MBS items billed at the time of appointment as recorded in the clinical information system in 2023–24 only. The MBS items used reflect relevant MBS Categories from 1 July 2017 until 30 June 2024 only (this outcome does not reflect recent changes to Chronic Disease Management items). It is uncertain the extent to which general practice data billing information reflects MBS data processed by Services Australia; further work to validate general practice data billing information would be useful but was not within the scope for this project. The following MBS items were used to count each care measure:

- Chronic disease management plan: 229, 721, 92024, 92055
- Chronic disease management review: 233, 732, 92028, 92059
- Team care arrangements: 230, 723, 92025, 92056
- Time tiered health assessment: 224, 701, 225, 703, 226, 705, 227, 707.

#### Attendances

Attendances was the number of visits by unique patients in 2023–24 only. Each unique identifier is assigned a flag indicating the number GP visits per unique identifier (UID) in 2023–24. It was assumed that individuals do not attend the GP more than once per day. Each UID can only have one discrete value representing their count of GP visits in 2023–24 (count flags are mutually exclusive). The project team identified some issues around validating the visit date and which data field most accurately represents the 'true' visit date; this issue is found in many administrative datasets. Further work to validate the visit date used in this project would be useful for the sector but is not within scope for this project.

#### Risk factors and health behaviours

Evaluation of risk factors and health behaviours does not account for all possible risk factors and behaviours. The study limited these analyses to a select group of highly prevalent risk factors and health behaviours commonly managed in primary care.

We counted the presence of risk factors using a number of data sources available in study data:

Patients can have multiple risk factors (risk factors are not mutually exclusive).

**Biomedical risk factors:** We counted a patient as having a risk factor if a value above our set thresholds appeared at any time (therefore, we assume a positive risk factor is the most up-to-date recording). The study team made this decision due to uncertainty about the timeliness and completeness in observations for clinical measurements like blood pressure. This issue impacts medical risk factors such as hypertension, high cholesterol and being overweight.

**Health behaviours:** Guided by experts, we made certain decisions about thresholds and how to count people with health behaviours.

Further work to validate threshold values, counting rules and how estimates compare to other populations and datasets would be valuable for the sector but was not within scope for this project.

- diagnostic codes in clinical history or reason for encounter tables (for example, whether a patient has a recorded diagnosis of hypertension)
- values recorded in clinical observations (for example, when a patient has their blood pressure recorded)
- data flags recorded in tables recording certain lifestyle factors (for example, when general practice staff records someone’s smoking status).

- Smoking: We counted people having a positive risk factor for smoking if their record contained ‘Smoker’ or ‘Ex-smoker’. Therefore, this makes it difficult to compare against other estimates as the definition used in the study crosses over with the number of people who are current smokers and the number of people who have previously smoked.
- Risky alcohol consumption: We counted a people as having risky alcohol consumption if their record contained a score of six and above on the AUDIT-C. This was guided by the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) guidelines, which compare well with National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) guidelines used by the AIHW’s National Drug Strategy Household Survey (NHMRC 2020; RACGP 2024).
- Illicit drug use: It is only possible to count people as having this risk factor through using diagnostic fields, meaning a patient record must include a diagnostic code for drug use in either their clinical history or reason for encounter (defined by ICPC-2 PLUS grouper P19).

#### Study time period issues

The restricted look back period of the study dataset (diagnostic information for this study commences 1 July 2017), may lead to underestimates in earlier years. Based on these undercounts in earlier years, only one year of outcomes were used in the study. It is recommended that future projects involve all historical data.

### Exploring the prevalence of conditions of interest in primary care methods

This analysis involved extracting 12-month cross-sectional prevalence counts for a number of select conditions included in the Australian Burden of Disease Study (ABDS). We counted patient records using diagnostic information found in WAPHA Primary Sense data for the calendar years 2018 to 2023. Analysis focused on the most recent year of data (2023) which was considered to have the highest quality and completeness of diagnosis and patient information.

Conditions for inclusion were selected based on criteria defined by the AIHW’s Burden of Disease and Mortality Unit and in consultation with subject matter experts, such as conditions which have previously utilised outdated primary care survey data or clinical studies for estimating prevalence, new conditions included for the first time in ABDS 2026, and conditions commonly managed by GPs for which prevalence is not fully captured in existing national data sources.

Conditions were defined with subject matter expertise by selecting relevant International Classification of Primary Care, second edition (ICPC-2), Australian extension (ICPC-2 PLUS) codes.

Unique patient records were selected where relevant ICPC-2 PLUS codes were found in either recorded clinical diagnoses (Past History field) or a coded reason for encounter field.

Condition counts were used in the calculation of prevalence rates using a denominator of all patients included in the Primary Sense data extract who had a visit in the reference year. Rates were then examined for their validity and plausibility against other known data sources on prevalence. Where rates were deemed suitable against other available information and data sources, they were incorporated into modelled national prevalence estimates for use in calculating burden of disease estimates for the 2026 Australian Burden of Disease Study (ABDS). For more information on how burden of disease estimates are calculated see the ABDS: Methods and supplementary material 2018.

# General practice care for people with a mental health condition

## Glossary

**General practice clinical information software:** A clinical information system (CIS) is a digital system clinicians use to record, manage and access a person's health information. It supports everyday tasks such as documenting care, reviewing medical history, managing medications and securely sharing information with other healthcare providers. General practice CIS is the software a GP and other practice staff use to record consultations and prescribe medicines. The most commonly used CISs are Best Practice (72.1%) and MedicalDirector (24.8%, including MD Clinical/Insights and Helix combined), followed by other software providers combined (3.1%). Primary Sense is compatible only with clinical information systems Best Practice and MedicalDirector.

**General practice extractor software:** General practice data extraction software safely and securely extracts clinical, demographic and billing data from general practice management systems. In Australia, common tools include POLAR (Population Level Analysis and Reporting), Primary Sense, PenCS (includes a separate general practice level software Clinical Assessment Tool (CAT)). These programs de-identify patient records to assist with quality improvement incentives and population health intelligence. This project uses Primary Sense data from Western Australia general practices that have been shared with WA Primary Health Alliance (WAPHA).
