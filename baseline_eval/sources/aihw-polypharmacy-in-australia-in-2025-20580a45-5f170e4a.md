# Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025

### Key findings

This report is the first release in a new series about polypharmacy. Polypharmacy is defined as the same person being on five or more medicines over a year to manage one or more health conditions. This report will explore how polypharmacy is distributed across age and sex, and what medicines were commonly dispensed in 2025.

- Cat. no: PHC 25

- In 2025, 7% of Australians (1.9 million people) were regularly dispensed 5 or more medicines at the same time
- Polypharmacy was most common among older people in 2025, but occurred across all age groups with similar rates by sex
- Mental health medicines were more common in younger people, while chronic disease medicines were more common in adults
- Around 1 in 9 of people experiencing polypharmacy were regularly dispensed 10 or more different medicines in 2025

# Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025

## Summary

Many people take more than one medicine to manage their health. For some people, this means taking 5 or more medicines at the same time, a situation known as polypharmacy. Polypharmacy can help people manage long-term and complex health conditions, however, taking multiple medicines can increase the risk of side effects, interactions between medicines and problems taking medicines as prescribed.

This report is the first in a new series on polypharmacy in Australia. It looks at patterns of polypharmacy by age and sex, and what medicines were commonly dispensed in 2025 using data from the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) and Repatriation Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (RPBS). Medicines supplied through private prescriptions are not included, so the results may underestimate the true extent of polypharmacy in Australia. See the Technical notes for more information about the data and methods used.

Future reports in this series will examine trends over time and differences across geographic areas.

## Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025, Introduction

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/medicines/polypharmacy-in-australia-in-2025/contents/introduction

# Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025

## Introduction

Polypharmacy refers to the concurrent use of multiple medicines and is defined as the regular use of 5 or more medicines at the same time (AIHW 2024; OECD 2025). It is a growing feature of health care in Australia, particularly among older people, many of whom require multiple medicines to manage chronic and complex health conditions (RACGP 2026).

While polypharmacy can be clinically appropriate and necessary for effective treatment, it is also associated with an increased risk of medication-related harm. These risks may include adverse drug reactions, interactions between medicines, and the chances of not taking medicines as prescribed. These risks increase as the number of medicines rises, highlighting the importance of regular medication reviews and appropriate prescribing (ACSQHC 2021).

Polypharmacy is defined as the regular use of 5 or more medicines over a year. The measure is designed to capture ongoing treatment to manage health conditions, rather than temporary medicine use. Medicines commonly prescribed for short-term conditions (such as antibiotics) are not included.

This report looks at age and sex patterns in polypharmacy in Australia in 2025, focusing on dispensing captured in the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) and Repatriation Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (RPBS) data. Medicines supplied through private prescriptions are not included and as a result, polypharmacy rates presented in this report may underestimate the true extent of polypharmacy in Australia. Information on individuals' medical conditions is not available in PBS/RPBS data, limiting interpretation of their health status. See the Technical notes for more information on the data sources.

## Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025, Why polypharmacy matters

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/medicines/polypharmacy-in-australia-in-2025/contents/why-polypharmacy-matters

# Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025

## Why polypharmacy matters

Understanding the extent and patterns of polypharmacy can provide insight into the complexity of medicine use across the population. In 2019, the World Health Organization (WHO) identified polypharmacy as a priority area for medication safety, recognising that the risk of medication-related harm generally increases as the number of medicines a person takes increases. Older people are particularly vulnerable due to age-related physiological changes, higher rates of multiple chronic conditions, and more frequent interactions with multiple prescribers and healthcare settings (WHO 2019).

### Risk of medication-related harm

Medication-related harm associated with polypharmacy may include:

Medication-related harm can reduce quality of life, contribute to avoidable healthcare use and lead to poorer health outcomes. The Fourth Australian Atlas of Healthcare Variation highlights the importance of safe and appropriate medicine use among older Australians, including the role of medication management reviews in identifying medicines that may no longer be needed, medicines that may interact, and opportunities to optimise treatment. As Australia's population ages and polypharmacy becomes more common, regular medication reviews, coordinated care and quality use of medicines will become increasingly important to reduce medicine-related harm and ensure medicines continue to provide more benefit than risk (ACSQHC 2021). The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC) leads national efforts to ensure medicines are used safely and appropriately, helping to reduce medication-related harm.

Monitoring polypharmacy can help identify population groups that may be at greater risk of medicine-related harm and support strategies (including those led by the ACSQHC) to improve the quality use of medicines. With an ageing population and a greater likelihood of chronic conditions and multimorbidity in older age groups (AIHW 2026), understanding patterns of polypharmacy can also inform health service planning, medication safety initiatives and the targeting of medication review services to people who are most likely to benefit.

- Adverse drug reactions – unwanted or harmful effects from medicines, such as dizziness, confusion, falls, bleeding or hospitalisation.
- Drug–drug interactions – when one medicine affects how another medicine works, potentially reducing effectiveness or increasing the risk of side effects.
- Difficulty managing multiple medicines – taking many medicines can make treatment regimens more complex, increasing the likelihood that medicines are not taken as prescribed (including missed doses or incorrect use).

### Polypharmacy affects 7% of Australians

In 2025, around 1.9 million people in Australia (7.0% of the Australian population) experienced polypharmacy, having been regularly dispensed 5 or more PBS/RPBS medicines during the year. The following analysis examines patterns of polypharmacy by age, sex and the number of medicines dispensed. It also identifies the medicines most commonly associated with polypharmacy within each age group.

#### Definitions

People were classified as **regular medicine users** if they were dispensed a medicine on at least 4 occasions during 2025.

**People experiencing polypharmacy** were defined as regular medicine users who were dispensed 5 or more PBS/RPBS medicines during 2025, where each medicine was dispensed on at least 4 occasions (referred to as ‘Polypharmacy (5+ medicines)’ in data figures).

**Regular medicine users taking 1–4 medicines** were defined as regular medicine users who were dispensed 1 to 4 PBS/RPBS medicines during 2025, where each medicine was dispensed on at least 4 occasions. Throughout this report, this group is referred to as ‘people taking 1–4 medicines’ (referred to as ‘1–4 medicines’ in data figures).

Refer to the Technical notes for more detail.

## Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025, Polypharmacy by age and sex

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/medicines/polypharmacy-in-australia-in-2025/contents/polypharmacy-by-age-and-sex

# Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025

## Polypharmacy by age and sex

### Polypharmacy is most common in older people

Polypharmacy increased markedly with age (Figure 1) and is most common among older people. Around 910,000 people aged 75 years and over were dispensed 5 or more PBS/RPBS medicines during the year, equivalent to 40% of the population aged 75 years and over. This compares to 20% of the population aged 65–74 years who were dispensed 5 or more medicines during the year, and 9.5% of the population aged 55–64 years.

Although polypharmacy was most common among older Australians, it occurred across all age groups. Rates were lowest among children aged 0–14 years, affecting 1,600 people (0.03% of the population aged 0–14 years), and were also low among people aged 15–24 years (0.2% of the population aged 15–24 years). The steady increase in polypharmacy rates with age is consistent with the increase of chronic conditions as age increases (AIHW 2026) and the increasing treatment complexity in older age groups.

As Australia's population continues to age, the number of people exposed to multiple medicines is likely to increase, with implications for medicine safety, appropriate medicine use, and healthcare utilisation. These findings highlight the importance of policies and programs that support the safe and appropriate use of medicines, including regular medication reviews, deprescribing where appropriate, monitoring medicine-related risks, and strategies to reduce potentially avoidable medicine-related harm.

### Polypharmacy rates are similar for males and females

Polypharmacy rates were similar overall between males and females in 2025 (Figure 1). Around 987,000 females were dispensed 5 or more PBS/RPBS medicines (7.1% of the female population), compared with 954,000 males (7.0% of the male population).

The pattern of polypharmacy by sex varied across age groups. For both males and females, polypharmacy rates increased steadily with age and was highest among people aged 75 years and over (42% of the male population and 39% of the female population, Figure 1). In younger age groups, between 15 to 54 years, females experienced polypharmacy at higher rates than males. This pattern changed from age 55 years onwards, when polypharmacy rates were higher among males than females. The largest difference was observed among people aged 65-74 years (22% of the male population compared to 18% of the female population, Figure 1). This is consistent with findings that older males are more likely to be living with 2 or more chronic conditions at the same time (multimorbidity) compared to older females (AIHW 2025) and therefore require more medication to manage these conditions. Overall, these findings suggest that age and the associated increase of chronic conditions (AIHW 2025) are the main drivers of polypharmacy, rather than sex.

## Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025, Polypharmacy by number of medicines dispensed

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/medicines/polypharmacy-in-australia-in-2025/contents/polypharmacy-by-number-of-medicines-dispensed

# Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025

## Polypharmacy by number of medicines dispensed

### Most are on 5 medicines

One-third (33%) of people experiencing polypharmacy in 2025 were dispensed only 5 PBS/RPBS medicines. The proportion of people decreased as the number of dispensed medicines increased, although a substantial minority of people experiencing polypharmacy were dispensed much higher numbers of medicines. Around 11% of people experiencing polypharmacy (219,000, representing 1% of the Australian population) were experiencing hyperpolypharmacy (where people were regularly dispensed 10 or more medicines, Davison et al. 2026).

### Number of medicines increases as people get older

The number of medicines dispensed generally increased with age (Figure 2). Among the younger age groups, most people experiencing polypharmacy were dispensed 5 medicines, with relatively few dispensed larger numbers of medicines. In contrast, older age groups had higher rates across all medicine counts, including 10 or more medicines. For people aged 65–74 years and 75 years and over, rates were relatively high across all medicine counts from 6 or more medicines onwards. This may reflect the fact that people in older age groups are more likely to be living with multiple chronic conditions (AIHW 2026).

Although each medicine may be clinically required, the risk of medication-related harm increases as the number of medicines used increases (ACSQHC 2021). This is particularly relevant for people prescribed 10 or more medicines. These individuals may have a greater risk of adverse drug reactions, drug-drug interactions and challenges associated with managing complex treatment regimens. These findings highlight the importance of medication reviews, coordinated care and quality use of medicine strategies to support the safe and appropriate use of medicines, particularly among people using higher numbers of medicines.

#### Figure 2: Polypharmacy by age and number of medicines dispensed in 2025

### Types of medicine change with age

Analysis by medicine group (based on Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical \[ATC\] classification Level 3 and 4 medicines) suggests that the dispensed medicines most commonly associated with polypharmacy in 2025 changed substantially by age (Figure 3). In younger age groups, polypharmacy was characterised by medicines for mental health and neurological conditions, particularly antidepressants, antipsychotics and psychostimulants. This is reflected in the fact that long-term mental health conditions that are most commonly diagnosed in childhood (such as ADHD and autism) tend to feature in multimorbidity among younger age groups (AIHW 2025).

In older adults, the pattern shifted towards medicines used to manage chronic physical health conditions, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, dyslipidaemia and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD). Antidepressants remained a common feature across most age groups, including in older adults.

## Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025, Comparison to people taking 1 to 4 medicines

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/medicines/polypharmacy-in-australia-in-2025/contents/comparison-to-people-taking-1-4-medicines

# Polypharmacy in Australia in 2025

## Comparison to people taking 1 to 4 medicines

While this report focuses on people experiencing polypharmacy, it also examines people who were regularly dispensed 1–4 medicines during the year. Analysing this group provides important context for understanding medicine use across the population and may help identify patterns among people approaching the threshold for polypharmacy. In particular, people regularly dispensed 3 or 4 medicines may be more likely to transition to polypharmacy over time. However, people taking 1–4 medicines and those experiencing polypharmacy may have very different underlying medical conditions and as the PBS/RPBS data does not contain information on underlying medical conditions, comparisons between these two groups should be made with care.

### Age patterns

In 2025, around 8 million people in Australia (29% of the population) were taking 1–4 medicines. Across all age groups, people taking 1–4 medicines outnumbered people experiencing polypharmacy (Figure 1). The proportion of people taking 1–4 medicines increased with age, peaking at 58% among people aged 65–74 years. The proportion was slightly lower among people aged 75 years and over (56% of the population aged 75 and over) but remained substantially higher than in younger age groups.

This pattern differed from polypharmacy, where the proportion of people experiencing polypharmacy increased steadily with age and was highest among people aged 75 years and over (Figure 1). The lower proportion of people taking 1–4 medicines among those aged 75 years and over may reflect a shift towards polypharmacy as people age and chronic conditions become more common (AIHW 2026). However, this interpretation should be treated cautiously, as longitudinal analysis would be required to determine whether people taking 1–4 medicines transition to polypharmacy (from those taking 3–4 medicines to five or more) over time.

### Differences between males and females

Among people taking 1–4 medicines, the proportion of females was generally higher than males between ages 15–64 (Figure 1). In contrast, among children aged 0–14 years, a higher proportion of males (8.1% of the male population) than females (4.8% of the female population) were taking 1–4 medicines. Differences between males and females narrowed from age 65 onwards. These patterns may be related to differences in multimorbidity, which is more common among females in younger and middle-aged groups, while males experience higher rates of multimorbidity in older age groups (AIHW 2025). These differences may contribute to varying patterns of medicine use across a person’s lifespan, potentially reflecting differences in the types and combinations of chronic conditions experienced by males and females (AIHW 2025).

### Number of medicines used

In 2025, among people taking 1–4 medicines, regular use of a single medicine was most common in younger age groups (Figure 2). The proportion of people taking one medicine peaked among people aged 55–64 years (21% of the population aged 55–64) before declining in older age groups. In contrast, the regular use of 3 or 4 medicines became more common among older age groups. This pattern may reflect the increasing complexity of health care needs and treatment with age and an increasing number of chronic conditions (AIHW 2026). Longitudinal analysis would be required to examine whether people taking larger numbers of medicines, particularly 3 or 4 medicines, transition to polypharmacy over time.

### Types of medicines used

Compared with people of the same age taking 1–4 medicines, the largest differences in medicine profiles among people experiencing polypharmacy were observed in younger age groups (Figure 3). Younger people taking 1–4 medicines were more commonly dispensed respiratory medicines, hormonal contraceptives and anti-acne treatments, alongside medicines for mental health and neurological conditions. In contrast, younger people experiencing polypharmacy had a greater concentration of mental health medicines. From age 45 onwards, the types of medicines dispensed became increasingly similar between the two groups, reflecting the growing role of chronic disease management in the older population.

### Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme and the Repatriation Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme

The Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) and the Repatriation Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (RPBS) are Australian Government programs that subsidise the cost of a wide range of medicines in Australia. The PBS began in 1948 and is available to current Medicare card holders as well as to overseas visitors from countries with reciprocal health care agreements with Australia. The RPBS was established in 1919 for returning Australian service people who had served in previous wars. Veterans, war widows and widowers, and their dependants are eligible for the RPBS if they have an assessed clinical need for the item and hold a Department of Veterans’ Affairs (DVA) gold, white or orange Veteran Card.

The PBS data collection contains information on prescription medicines that qualify for a benefit under the *National Health Act 1953* and for which a claim has been processed. The database comprises information about PBS and RPBS scripts and payments, patients, prescribers and dispensing pharmacies.

### Scope of PBS statistics

PBS statistics only include prescription medicines that are listed on the Schedule of Pharmaceutical Benefits (PBS Schedule) and have had a claim processed. They do not include:

- private prescriptions, off-label prescribing, over-the-counter medicines
- medicines supplied to public hospital in-patients (except New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory)
- PBS Opiate Dependence Treatment Program before 1 July 2023
- any PBS medicines supplied under special arrangements that are not processed through PBS online
- information on reasons for prescribing, medical conditions, prescribed dosage, frequency of administration and intended duration
- information on how the person uses the dispensed medicine once they have received it.

### Populations

Population statistics are sourced from the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) Estimated Resident Population (ERP) statistics as at 30 June.

In relation to services per resident of an area, the denominator is the total ERP of the area, as at the previous 30 June.

- For any month of the first half of a calendar year, the ERP is as at 30 June of previous year.
- For any month of the second half of the year, the ERP is as at 30 June of the year.
- The most current ERP is used when the relevant ERP has not been released.

### Definitions

Polypharmacy is defined as a person taking 5 or more medicines (based on Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical \[ATC\] classification Level 4 medicines) concurrently within a given year (2025 for this report). This is the OECD definition of polypharmacy (OECD 2025).

The following approach was used:

Medicine use may be underestimated due to the PBS/RPBS data collection lacking information on private prescriptions, off-label prescribing, etc.

The polypharmacy rate and the rate of people taking 1 to 4 medicines (per 100,000 people) are defined as:

Number of people experiencing polypharmacy in 2025 divided by the relevant Australian population in 2025 (based on ERP) x 100,000

and

Number of people taking 1 to 4 medicines in 2025 divided by the relevant Australian population in 2025 (based on ERP) x 100,000.

People are included in counts and rates for age and/or sex only if sex is recorded as male or female and/or a valid date of birth is available. As a result, totals by age and/or sex may not align with overall totals. For medicines prescribed exclusively to one sex, the denominator used in rates is not limited to the corresponding sex-specific population. Note that in lower age groups, numbers are small and statistics may be subject to volatility.

The percentage of people regularly taking medicines in each age group was calculated as the proportion of the relevant people in that age group who were dispensed at least one medicine from the relevant ATC Level 3 or 4 group during 2025. Percentages do not sum to 100%, as people may be dispensed medicines from multiple ATC groups. Note that an ATC code reflects a medicine’s primary therapeutic use and classification. While many medicines have multiple indications, the ATC system generally classifies them according to their main therapeutic use.
