## About

Health system spending attributable to disease and injury increased by $48.4 billion between 2015–16 and 2024–25 after adjusting for inflation, rising from $146.7 billion in 2015–16 to $195.1 billion in constant 2024–25 prices. This decomposition analysis shows that most of the growth was associated with population growth, higher expenditure per service and population ageing. These findings help distinguish growth linked to demographic change from growth linked to service use, disease burden and the cost of delivering care.

The results show that spending growth was not uniform across the health system. The relative importance of each driver varied across areas of expenditure, disease and injury groups, age groups and sex, highlighting where different pressures are shaping expenditure. The analysis brings together AIHW disease expenditure data, Australian Burden of Disease Study estimates and ABS population data. Expenditure is presented in constant 2024–25 prices to remove the effect of general price inflation. Overall, 80.8% of total disease-attributable expenditure between 2015–16 and 2024–25 could be included in the decomposition analysis, with exclusions mainly relating to conditions or expenditure groups that could not be linked to burden of disease estimates or did not meet data completeness requirements. In this article, ‘drivers’ refers to factors used to allocate observed expenditure change under the decomposition. Results can only establish an association and do not imply independent causal effects.

## Summary

Disease-attributable expenditure increased by $48.4 billion between 2015–16 and 2024–25, after adjusting for inflation. Of this increase, $41.7 billion, or 86.1%, could be included in the decomposition analysis. Results from the decomposition showed that:

Population growth was the largest contributor to increased spending, followed by expenditure per service and population ageing. The relative importance of the drivers varied across areas of expenditure, conditions, age groups and sex. These results represent estimated contributions under the decomposition model and should not be interpreted as independent causal effects.

- population growth contributed $18.5 billion, or 44.3%;
- higher expenditure per service contributed $14.0 billion, or 33.6%
- population ageing contributed $6.7 billion, or 16.0%
- changes in disease burden contributed $2.8 billion, or 6.7% and
- changes in service use per disability-adjusted life year (DALY) reduced expenditure by $0.3 billion or -0.6%, partly offsetting growth from the other factors.

## Introduction

Health system spending on disease and injury changes over time because of shifts in the size and structure of the population, changes in the level of disease burden, changes in how often health services are used, and changes in the cost of delivering those services. Understanding the relative contribution of these drivers can help explain why spending has increased and where growth has been concentrated across the health system.

This article uses a growth decomposition approach to separate the change in disease-attributed health expenditure between 2015–16 and 2024–25 into five components: population size, population age and sex structure, disease burden, service use per DALY, and expenditure per service. A DALY, or disability-adjusted life year, is a measure of overall disease burden that combines the years of healthy life lost due to living with illness or injury and dying prematurely.

The analysis uses AIHW disease expenditure data along with Australian Burden of Disease Study estimates and ABS population estimates (ABS 2026). Expenditure is converted to 2024–25 dollars using health expenditure price deflators (AIHW 2026a) so that changes reflect real growth rather than general price inflation. The results describe broad patterns in expenditure growth and should be interpreted as estimated contributions rather than direct causal effects.

## Technical notes

#### Decomposition methods overview

This section outlines the technical methods used to analyse the drivers of disease expenditure using a five factor Das Gupta decomposition analysis. Expenditure included in the analysis covers the ten-year period from 2015–16 to 2024–25 from the AIHW disease expenditure database. The decomposition analysis breaks down changes in total health spending across five factors: population size, population age/sex structure, disease burden (DALYs), service use per DALY, and expenditure per service. The analysis was based on an extended Das Gupta (1993) framework for a five-factor decomposition, incorporating an internal consistency adjustment for multiple periods. The disease expenditure database (AIHW unpublished), the Australian Burden of Disease Study database (AIHW 2024), and ABS population estimates (ABS 2026) were the primary data sources used to construct the analysis.

#### Disease Expenditure database

Expenditure data was sourced from the AIHW disease expenditure database. For each financial year from 2015–16 to 2024–25, expenditure records were extracted from the following areas of expenditure:

Data contains disease attribution using the Australian Burden of Disease Study 2024 (AIHW 2024) disease classification, age group (19 five-year age groups from \<1 year to 85 years and over), sex (males, females), financial year, expenditure (in current prices), and a count of services or separations. Records with zero expenditure or zero service counts were excluded prior to analysis.

For further details on what is included in the AIHW Disease expenditure database refer to the *Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25: Overview of analysis and methodology* report \[PDF, 797KB\] (AIHW 2026b).

- Public hospital admitted patient services - includes all admitted separations occurring in public hospitals (both public and private patients).
- Private hospital admitted patient services - includes all admitted separations occurring in private hospitals (both public and private patients).
- Public hospital emergency department services - includes all presentations to public hospital emergency departments.
- Public hospital non-admitted patient (outpatient) services - includes patients attending specialist, diagnostic, allied health and other public hospital outpatient clinics.
- General practitioner services - includes all general practitioner related MBS claims.
- Allied health and other services - includes all allied health and other services related MBS claims.
- Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) expenditure - includes all prescriptions within the PBS database.
- Dental expenditure - includes dental expenditure estimates sourced from HEA.
- Specialist services - includes all out of hospital specialist related MBS claims.
- Pathology services - includes all pathology related MBS claims.
- Medical imaging services - includes all medical imaging related MBS claims.

#### What spending is included?

The disease expenditure estimates include spending from all sources of funds - government, private health insurance and patient co-payments - where that spending can be allocated to disease, age and sex.

Some spending is excluded, such as some out-of-pocket spending for privately provided physiotherapy or psychology services, or costs borne through the aged care system (where management of conditions such as dementia or stroke may occur).

#### Australian Burden of Disease data

Burden of Disease (BoD) estimates were sourced from the Australian Burden of Disease Study 2024 (AIHW 2024) and included disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years lived with disability (YLD), and years of life lost (YLL). The BoD data are provided for discrete reference years between 2003 to 2024. To generate estimates for all financial years, logarithmic interpolation was applied between the available reference years.

For each combination of disease, sex and age group, known BoD estimates were added to a structured dataset containing all required time periods, with missing values estimated using log-linear interpolation between adjacent known data points. Where a variable takes a zero or negative value at either end-point, linear interpolation was substituted to avoid computational error. Mid year interpolated estimates were used to represent total DALYs in a given financial year. For the 2024–25 period, a log-linear extrapolation was used to estimate BoD data for this single period as the BoD data last publication period was for the year 2024. Prior to merging BoD data with expenditure data, BoD age groups were harmonised with the disease expenditure database age group structure. Lastly, only data from 2015–16 onwards was used within the final BoD data file.

Population estimates were sourced from the ABS’ December Estimated Resident Population (ERP) (ABS 2026). Single-year-of-age population counts were grouped into 19 five-year age groups to align with the disease expenditure and BoD age group classification structure.

Financial year population denominators were constructed by matching each financial year to the corresponding calendar year ERP (for example, 2024–25 uses the December 2024 ERP). For the decomposition analysis, only Australian totals were used. Total Australian population estimates (used as the population size factor) and age-sex-specific subpopulation estimates (used to create the population structure fraction and burden of disease rates) were then merged to the expenditure dataset.

Expenditure was converted from current prices to constant 2024–25 prices to adjust for inflation within health expenditure using price deflators sourced from the AIHW Health Expenditure Australia (HEA) database. Separate deflators are applied to each broad area of expenditure, as set out in Table 1.

Constant-price expenditure is calculated as current price expenditure divided by the deflator value expressed as an index with base 100. For the most recent financial year (2024–25), the deflator base equals 100, such that current and constant prices are identical.

Where a factor equals zero, the record is excluded from the decomposition, as the multiplicative identity cannot be evaluated.

- N(t) is the total Australian population in year t (population size factor)
- P(a,s,t) is the proportion of the total population in age-sex group (a,s) in year t (population age-sex structure factor)
- R(d,a,s,t) is the burden of disease rate (DALYs per person) for disease d in age-sex group (a,s) in year t (disease burden factor)
- U(d,c,a,s,t) is the number of health services per DALY for disease d and area of expenditure c in age-sex group (a,s) in year t (service use) and
- C(d,c,a,s,t) is the constant-price expenditure per service for disease d and area of expenditure c in age-sex group (a,s) in year t (expenditure per service factor).

- Population size: The total Australian population in each year, measured using Estimated Resident Population (ERP) data produced by the Australian Bureau of Statistics.
- Population age structure: The distribution of the population across age and sex groups, calculated as the age‑ and sex‑specific ERP expressed as a proportion of the total Australian population in each year.
- Disease burden: The level of disease burden in Australia, measured using disability‑adjusted life year (DALY) rates per person for each age, sex and disease group in each year (estimates sourced from the AIHW Burden of Disease Unit). As DALY estimates are not available for every year, missing rates and counts were derived using logarithmic interpolation between reported estimates to produce a complete time series.
- Service use per DALY: The intensity of health service use, measured as the number of health services per unit of disease burden. This was calculated by dividing age, sex, disease and area‑of‑expenditure specific service counts by the corresponding age, sex and disease‑specific DALY counts for each year.
- Expenditure per service: Expenditure per health service in constant prices, calculated as total constant price expenditure divided by service or separation counts within each age, sex, disease and area of expenditure group for each year.

#### Time series assembly

Expenditure data was extracted for each financial year from 2015–16 to 2024–25 using a standardised import procedure. For each year, data from all ten areas of expenditure were imported, standardised to common variable names and age group, sex, and cost source labels, and then aggregated by year, area of expenditure, ABDS2024 code, sex, and age group. The aggregated annual datasets were then appended to form a single combined time series dataset. Records with missing ABDS2024 disease codes were identified and a count was produced. Records with zero expenditure or zero service counts were excluded from all subsequent processing.

The decomposition was restricted to strata with comparable and valid data across the full analysis period. A stratum was defined as a unique combination of disease, area of expenditure, age group and sex. First, only strata represented in all ten financial years from 2015–16 to 2024–25 were retained. This ensured that changes over time were based on a consistent set of strata.

The retained records were then checked to ensure that all five decomposition factors were non-missing and non-zero. Records that did not meet this requirement were excluded because the decomposition’s multiplicative identity could not be evaluated for them. At each stage, the expenditure and service counts retained and excluded were summarised to assess the coverage of the final dataset and identify potential bias arising from the exclusions (see data Table 2 in data downloads).

Finally, to ensure that the exclusions did not disproportionately affect any cost source–condition subgroup, only combinations for which both total expenditure and total service counts remained at or above 90% of their original values were retained (see Table 3 in the data downloads).

After developing the decomposition analysis framework, the analysis was run using DALYs, YLDs and YLLs to assess the best measure of disease burden. Final outputs were compared in addition to a review of expenditure exclusions caused by the choice of disease burden measure. After review, DALYs were selected as the preferred measure for the analysis as this facilitated the least amount of expenditure lost after processing the input data.

The primary output of the decomposition analysis is a dataset of factor effects at the stratum level, containing one row per combination of disease, area of expenditure, age group, and sex. Five effect variables are produced for each stratum, corresponding to the effect factors: population size, population age structure, disease burden (DALYs), service use per DALY, and expenditure per service. Effects are expressed in constant 2024–25 Australian dollars. The results of this analysis are available in the Data section within Table 1.

The results should be interpreted with the following scope and limitations in mind. Most importantly, the analysis covers around 81% of disease-attributed expenditure and does not include expenditure that could not be linked to burden of disease estimates or did not have complete data across the full analysis period.

- Burden of disease estimates are available for discrete reference years only and are interpolated to financial year mid-points for all other years. The logarithmic interpolation method assumes smooth monotonic change in disease burden between reference years and may not accurately reflect abrupt shifts in disease incidence or case fatality.
- A number of conditions with substantial expenditure, including COVID-19 and conditions classified under Well care, Examination and Observation NEC, and Treatment of risk factors, were excluded as they could not be linked to burden of disease estimates or were outside the scope of the analysis. Results therefore reflect spending growth for the included portion of disease-attributed expenditure.
- The analysis only includes strata that contained data for all factors across all ten years. As a result, some rare conditions or newly classified conditions may be excluded, slightly reducing the amount of expenditure covered.
- The Das Gupta framework assumes that the five factors are multiplicatively separable. The internal consistency adjustment mitigates path-dependency in multi-period summation but does not eliminate all cross-factor interactions. Results should therefore be interpreted as approximate attributions rather than strict causal decompositions.
