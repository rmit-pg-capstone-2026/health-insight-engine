## Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25, About

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/health-welfare-expenditure/health-system-spending-disease-injury-2024-25/contents/about

# Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25

### Key findings

*Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024*–*25*, analyses Australia’s national health spending to provide additional detail about the people receiving care and the conditions and diseases being treated.

In this report, 2024–25 data is presented for each of the 17 Australian Burden of Disease Study (ABDS) groups and 220 conditions within these groups. Spending for well care, the treatment of risk factors and examination and observation is also included. Total spending, per 100,000 population spending and cost per service is analysed by state, area of expenditure, age group and sex. In addition, this report includes spending by remoteness, socioeconomic status, spending for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander (First Nations) people, cancer screening and research. It looks at the impact of including spending on community and public health programs specifically targeted at diseases or disease groups on the ranking of burden of disease group spending. The focus of this report is data for 2024–25 but also includes comparisons with historical data back to 2013–14.

## Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25, Summary

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/health-welfare-expenditure/health-system-spending-disease-injury-2024-25/contents/summary

# Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25

## Summary

Spending by disease and injury increased by $13.9 billion to $195.1 billion in 2024–25, up from $181.2 billion in 2023–24.

Of the $195.1 billion (around 71% of recurrent health spending as reported in Health expenditure Australia 2024-25), close to two-thirds was for hospital services ($125.0 billion), over one-quarter for primary health care ($52.3 billion) with the remaining spending for referred medical services ($17.7 billion).

In 2024–25 the highest spending was for cancer ($21.7 billion), followed by cardiovascular diseases ($18.4 billion), and musculoskeletal disorders ($17.5 billion). Cancer also had the highest burden, or human cost of disease in 2024, with mental health conditions and substance use disorders ranking second.

When disease-related community and public health program expenditure was included, the ranking of some disease groups changed. Mental health conditions and substance use disorders had the largest change, moving from fifth highest burden of disease group to third highest in terms of spending.

Spending on cancer more than doubled from $9.1 billion in 2013–14 to $21.7 billion in 2024–25 (unadjusted for inflation) and has been the highest ranked disease group in terms of spending since 2017–18. In 2024–25, spending on non-melanoma skin cancer ranked higher than spending on any other specific cancer with $2.12 billion spent on this condition, followed by breast cancer with $2.09 billion and lung cancer with $2.04 billion.

Cancer, cardiovascular diseases and musculoskeletal disorders, were the top 3 burden of disease groups in all but one year over the period between 2013–14 and 2024–25, accounting for close to one-third of spending each year.

Spending on chronic conditions (often referred to as long-term health conditions), accounted for around $107.6 billion in 2024–25, over half (55%) of all disease spending in 2024–25. Over the period 2013–14 to 2024–25, total spending on disease and injury grew $94.1 billion (in current prices), of which $56.8 billion (60%) was due to an increase in spending for chronic conditions.

The conditions with the highest spending in 2024–25 were injuries from falls ($5.7 billion), osteoarthritis ($5.2 billion) and back pain and problems ($4.3 billion). This was followed by spending on coronary heart disease ($4.0 billion) and lower respiratory infections (which includes influenza and pneumonia) ($3.8 billion).

In 2024–25, there was higher spending for females ($103.3 billion) than males ($91.5 billion). For both males and females, spending peaked in the 75–79 years age group.

There was $15.0 billion spent on well care in 2024–25. The majority of this spending (81%) was for routine dental checkups and cleaning and pregnancy and postpartum care.

Across Australia, cost per service was consistently higher, across all burden of disease groups combined, for people living in *Remote* and *Very remote* areas than for those living in *Major cities* between 2013–14 and 2024–25.

For the first time, this report presents expenditure on disease and injury by socioeconomic status, allowing spending patterns to be compared across areas with different levels of socioeconomic disadvantage. Across all disease groups and areas of spending, cost per service was consistently higher for people living in the most disadvantaged areas than for those living in the least disadvantaged areas between 2013–14 and 2024–25.

Hospitalisation expenditure by Indigenous status is included for the first time in this report. Across all disease groups combined, average hospitalisation expenditure was higher for First Nations people than for non-Indigenous people, at $5,683 per person compared with $3,508 per person.

This report uses a range of modelling techniques to apportion health spending to population groups based on age and sex, and to Australian Burden of Disease Study (ABDS) groups and to conditions within those groups.

Total spending, per 100,000 population spending and cost per service is analysed by state, area of expenditure, age group and sex. Whilst findings in this report are based on estimates (rather than direct observations) these data provide important insights into the nature and drivers of health spending, such as how an ageing population affects health spending.

A related report, Growth decomposition of health system spending on disease and injury in Australia, explores the factors contributing to growth in disease expenditure, including changes in population size, population ageing, disease burden, service use and expenditure per service.

# Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25

## Introduction

The AIHW Disease expenditure database takes data from Australia’s National Health Accounts, that forms the base of reporting for the AIHW Health expenditure Australia (HEA) report series (latest report, AIHW 2026a) and further examines the data to understand more about the people receiving care and the diseases and conditions being managed. The Disease expenditure database contains spending estimates for 17 Australian Burden of Disease Study (ABDS) groups and the 220 conditions within those groups, by age group and sex for each of the following broad and detailed areas of spending:

Spending on health prevention (through public and community health programs as defined in Health expenditure Australia 2024–25) is currently not included in the main AIHW Disease Expenditure database. However, the Comparison of disease expenditure and disease burden section examines the impact of including disease related community and public health program spending, on the ranking of disease groups.

When interpreting findings, expenditure should not be read as a direct measure of disease burden, health system priority or the effectiveness of care. Higher or lower spending can reflect many factors, including the number of people receiving services, the types and costs of treatments available, patterns of service use, disease severity, and the way spending can be allocated to specific conditions.

For changes in expenditure over time, growth should also be understood as reflecting multiple drivers. These can include population growth, population ageing, changes in the number and types of services used, changes in how care is delivered, new technologies or treatments, and changes in prices. Growth in spending should therefore not be interpreted as being solely due to worsening health outcomes or increasing disease burden. A related report, *Growth decomposition of health system spending on disease and injury in Australia*, explores the factors contributing to growth in disease expenditure, including changes in population size, population ageing, disease burden, service use and expenditure per service.

While spending for the national cancer screening programs for breast, cervical and bowel cancer is not included as part of the Disease Expenditure database, there is a separate section in this report for spending related to cancer screening.

Similarly, health research is not included as part of the Disease Expenditure database, but there is a separate section for research in this report that reflects on National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) expenditure for disease, research and health areas presented according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). This section also makes reference to the expenditure included as part of the Medical Research Future Fund (MRFF).

For further details on what is included in the AIHW Disease expenditure database compared to the AIHW Health expenditure database, refer to Table 2.2 from the accompanying methods report, *Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25: Overview of analysis and methodology* (AIHW 2026b).

### What spending is included?

The disease expenditure estimates include spending from all sources of funds - government, private health insurance and patient co-payments - where that spending can be allocated to disease, age and sex.

Some spending is excluded, such as some out-of-pocket spending for privately provided physiotherapy or psychology services, or costs borne through the aged care system (where management of conditions such as dementia or stroke may occur).

For data sources, patients' sex was recorded as male, female or other, not reported or unknown. This may be based on what the patient selected, or how staff completed the record. It may also be based on an existing record for the patient, which may no longer reflect how they identify.

It is important to note that it is not known if the people completing these records interpreted sex to mean sex at birth or gender identity.

This report uses the terms 'male’ and ‘female', but it should be noted that some participants may not identify with these terms. Where sex was reported as other, not reported or unknown, the data has been included as part of the spending for ‘Total Persons’.

### How do we measure disease costs?

The cost of disease is not just financial. Being unwell or suffering from a health condition has other effects on quality of life, affecting people’s ability to work or do the activities they enjoy. The spending estimates in this report do not include direct costs from outside of the health care sector or estimates of the indirect costs due to illness.

How much is spent on treating, managing, or preventing conditions in financial terms can be influenced by a range of factors such as the cost and availability of effective treatments, and disease prevalence. As such, the disease expenditure estimates in this report do not necessarily reflect the incidence or prevalence of those conditions, or the full ‘burden’, or human cost. Refer to the Comparison of disease expenditure and disease burden section in this report and AIHW 2024b for further information.

It is not feasible (or appropriate) to allocate some forms of health spending to specific diseases. For example, administration expenditure and capital expenditure are generally unable to be attributed to any particular condition. In addition, most community and public health programs, which support the treatment and prevention of many conditions, do not have sufficient data to allocate to conditions. Therefore, the disease expenditure estimates in this publication are not directly comparable with estimates published in the AIHW’s Health expenditure Australia reports (which cover all health spending) (AIHW 2026a). Refer to Figure 1 in this report and Table 2.2 in the accompanying methods paper (AIHW 2026b), available from the Related material page, for more detailed information on the inclusions and exclusions and for further details on the estimation methods, scope of data included, and comparability to previous studies.

Health spending in Australia is generally managed through particular funding programs such as the National Health Reform Agreement (NHRA), MBS or PBS. Often the relationship under these schemes between the spending, the particular diseases or conditions being managed, and the demographic characteristics of the people whose care the spending is for, is complex. It can be difficult, for example, to precisely identify for a hospital stay involving someone suffering from a number of ailments and including a range of procedures and treatments, which expenses were related to which conditions. Health spending is also often associated with the management of symptoms and issues for which there is no specific diagnosis (for example, someone attending an Emergency Department (ED) with abdominal pain for which no specific cause can be identified).

The aim of this report is to use a range of modelling techniques to apportion health spending to population groups based on age, sex, and to disease expenditure groups using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) and the AIHW’s Australian Burden of Disease Study (ABDS) conditions as far as is possible. Due to data availability, allocated spending is skewed towards activity in hospitals, and estimates should be interpreted with this in mind.

This disease expenditure study largely draws upon previously published methods, the most recent being Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2023–24: Overview of analysis and methodology report (AIHW 2025a). There were however updates made to the MBS and PBS mapping files that allocate expenditure to conditions to improve some disease mappings. Within the MBS mapping file, MBS items can now be mapped to up to ten ABDS conditions/groups. The MBS and PBS analysis was updated for all years in the time series. Revisions were also made to public and private hospitals total expenditure to align with updated estimates in the AIHW health expenditure database.

Data in this report should be used and are not directly comparable to data in earlier reports (AIHW 2019, AIHW 2021, AIHW 2022, AIHW 2023, 2024c, 2025a). This report also includes comparisons with historical data (using these updated methods) so there is a consistent time series for 2013–14 to 2024–25. Refer to the Technical notes and the accompanying methods report (AIHW 2026b). The methods report is available from the Related material section.

### What is new in the 2024–25 disease expenditure study?

Throughout this report, expenditure estimates are also presented on a cost per service basis where allowable. Services within each detailed area of expenditure are defined as either a hospital separation, a recorded MBS service, or a prescribed script. Presenting cost per service can help to understand cost differences across all stratifications within the disease expenditure database providing a more accurate picture of per-service health expenditure.

There is an expanded section on remoteness and a new section on socioeconomic status that contain several interactive data visualisations across the full time series. Within each section, expenditure is presented between 2013–14 and 2024–25 for different areas of expenditure, disease groups, sex and states. Additionally, interactive maps allow for inspection of expenditure estimates across regions in Australia. The visualisations are presented as total spending, cost per service and spending per 100,000 population.

A new section Spending on hospitalisations for First Nations people has been added to the report for 2024–25. This section presents estimates of hospitalisation expenditure for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander (First Nations) people in 2024–25. It compares expenditure for First Nations people and non-Indigenous people across burden of disease groups, age groups and potentially preventable hospitalisations, using total and per person expenditure to highlight where differences in spending are greatest. This section includes several visualisations and tables that are available for download from the Data section that correspond to tables for measure 3.21 from the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Performance Framework (updated to 2024–25).

Across many disease groups, there is a substantial amount of government expenditure within public and community health that can be directly allocated to specific burden of disease groups, such as public funding for infectious disease prevention, or funding for mental health services. When comparing overall disease expenditure against burden of disease groups, to allow for a more accurate representation of total disease related expenditure, public and community health expenditure has been added into this comparison section to see the impact of its inclusion. See Comparison of disease expenditure and disease burden section.

The Spending on cancer screening section has been expanded. This section now presents spending on Australia’s national population-based cancer screening programs from 2013–14 to 2024–25. It includes total spending for breast, cervical and bowel cancer screening programs. In addition, national participation rates have been used to develop sex and age specific estimates for the screening program expenditure.

For estimates of how much was spent through the health system in 2024–25 for each case of a disease or condition that was known to be prevalent in the community in that year and spending attributable to potentially avoidable risk factors such as being overweight, physical inactivity and tobacco use in that year, refer Health system spending per case of disease and for certain risk factors (AIHW 2025b).

## Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25, Spending on disease by Australian Burden of Disease groups

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/health-welfare-expenditure/health-system-spending-disease-injury-2024-25/contents/spending-on-disease-by-australian-burden-of-diseas

# Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25

## Spending on disease by Australian Burden of Disease groups

Burden of disease measures the impact of diseases and injuries on a population. It combines the years of healthy life lost due to living with ill health (non-fatal burden) with the years of life lost due to dying prematurely (fatal burden). The Australian Burden of Disease Study (ABDS) includes estimates of disease burden due to 220 diseases and injuries in Australia. In this disease hierarchy, each disease is allocated to one of 17 disease groups. The burden of disease groups contain related diseases or conditions – such as cardiovascular diseases, gastrointestinal disorders, or injuries – and one alternative reporting disease group (nature of injury vs injury by external cause). These groups are listed in the figures below.

Not all health spending is directly related to a burden of disease condition or group. The AIHW Disease Expenditure database also incorporates three additional groups – well care, the treatment of risk factors and examination and observation not elsewhere classified (NEC). These groups include medical treatments for risk factors, long term outcomes of some conditions, well care and prevention causes, and other interventions and symptoms that are reported as the reasons for provision of health care. Refer to the Technical notes in this web report and to the accompanying methods report, Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25: Overview of analysis and methodology, available in the Related material section, for further information on the inclusions for these additional groups.

In 2024–25, cancer and other neoplasms was the burden of disease group with the highest spending ($21.7 billion), followed by cardiovascular diseases ($18.4 billion), and musculoskeletal disorders ($17.5 billion).

## Spending by socioeconomic status

This section presents estimates of health system spending on disease and injury by socioeconomic status from 2013–14 to 2024–25. Socioeconomic status is an important way of understanding differences in health, health service use and health system expenditure across the Australian population. People living in areas of greater socioeconomic disadvantage often experience higher rates of illness and injury, greater exposure to health risk factors, and barriers to accessing timely and appropriate care. Examining disease spending by socioeconomic status helps show how health system expenditure varies across the social gradient and can provide evidence to support policies aimed at reducing health inequalities.

In this report, socioeconomic status is based on the Australian Bureau of Statistics’ Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA), using the Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage (IRSD). The IRSD summarises information about the relative economic and social conditions of people and households within an area, including factors such as income, employment and education. A low IRSD score indicates an area of relatively greater disadvantage, while a high score indicates an area with relatively less disadvantage.

Results are reported by socioeconomic area quintiles. These quintiles divide the population into 5 groups, each containing about 20% of the population, based on the socioeconomic ranking of the area in which people live. People living in the 20% of areas with the greatest level of disadvantage are described as living in the lowest socioeconomic areas, or the most disadvantaged areas. People living in the 20% of areas with the least level of disadvantage are described as living in the highest socioeconomic areas, or the least disadvantaged areas.

Because SEIFA is an area-based measure, it reflects the overall socioeconomic characteristics of geographic areas rather than the socioeconomic circumstances of individuals. Not everyone living in a disadvantaged area is disadvantaged, and not everyone living in a less disadvantaged area is advantaged. Findings should therefore be interpreted as differences in disease spending for people living in areas with different levels of relative socioeconomic disadvantage.

When interpreting expenditure by socioeconomic status, it is important to consider the measure being used. Total spending is influenced by the number of people living in each socioeconomic area group and the volume of services provided. Spending per 100,000 population can help compare expenditure across socioeconomic groups with different population sizes, while cost per service provides an indication of average expenditure for each service. Differences in spending may reflect factors such as differences in health need, disease prevalence, service use, access to care, severity of illness, service mix and the types and costs of treatments provided.

The analysis compares spending patterns across socioeconomic area quintiles and over time, including changes between 2013–14 and 2024–25. Findings are presented for total expenditure, expenditure per 100,000 population and cost per service. These comparisons can help identify disease groups where spending is concentrated among people living in more disadvantaged areas, as well as conditions where spending is more evenly distributed across socioeconomic groups.

Across Australia, cost per service was consistently higher for people living in the most disadvantaged areas than for those living in the least disadvantaged areas between 2013–14 and 2024–25, across all burden of disease groups combined and all areas of expenditure.

# Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25

## Spending on hospitalisations for First Nations people

This section presents estimates of hospitalisation expenditure for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander (First Nations) people in 2024–25. It compares expenditure for First Nations people and non-Indigenous people across burden of disease groups, age groups and potentially preventable hospitalisations, using total and per person expenditure to highlight where differences in spending are greatest.

When interpreting these findings, it is important to note that this section only includes expenditure for hospitalisations and does not capture spending on primary health care or referred medical services. Differences in hospitalisation expenditure may reflect differences in health need, disease prevalence and severity, access to timely and appropriate care, patterns of hospital service use, length of stay, care complexity and the cost of providing hospital services. Higher expenditure should therefore not be interpreted as a direct measure of disease burden, health system priority or the effectiveness of care.

Hospitalisation expenditure patterns should also be interpreted alongside information on overall disease burden. Burden of disease measures the health impact of diseases and injuries, including both years of healthy life lost due to illness or injury and years of life lost due to premature death. In 2022, First Nations people lost 317,333 years of healthy life due to disease and injury, or 311 disability-adjusted life years (DALY) per 1,000 people. Mental health conditions and substance use disorders contributed the largest share of total burden (20%), followed by injuries (13%), cardiovascular diseases (11%), cancer and other neoplasms (11%) and respiratory diseases (7.7%). Together, the 5 disease groups contributing most to burden accounted for 62% of total burden among First Nations people (AIHW 2026).

Hospitalisation expenditure captures only the hospital care component of health system spending, so disease groups with higher hospitalisation expenditure for First Nations people are not necessarily the same disease groups that contribute most to overall disease burden. Differences between expenditure and burden may reflect the extent to which conditions require hospital care, patterns of service use, access to care outside hospital, disease severity and survival.

### How to interpret findings in this section

The visualisations in this section present hospitalisation expenditure in several ways. Total expenditure shows the overall amount spent on hospitalisations for each group or condition. This measure is affected by both the number of hospitalisations and the cost of those hospitalisations, so larger population groups or more common conditions will generally have higher total expenditure.

Expenditure per person shows average hospitalisation expenditure for each person in the population group, not the average cost of a hospital stay. It is calculated by dividing total hospitalisation expenditure by the estimated population for that group. This helps compare expenditure between First Nations people and non-Indigenous people, including where the two groups have different population sizes.

Higher expenditure per person should not be interpreted as meaning better access to hospital care or better health outcomes. In many cases, it may reflect higher rates of illness, injury and hospitalisation among First Nations people, as well as differences in health need, disease severity, service use, and the complexity or cost of hospital care.

Ratios show relative differences between groups. A ratio of 1 means expenditure per person was the same for First Nations people and non-Indigenous people. A ratio above 1 means expenditure per person was higher for First Nations people; for example, a ratio of 2 means expenditure per person was twice as high.

Relative differences compare expenditure per person between First Nations people and non-Indigenous people as a ratio. They show how many times higher or lower expenditure per person was for one group compared with the other. For example, a ratio of 3 means expenditure per person was 3 times as high for First Nations people as for non-Indigenous people. A high relative difference can occur even when the dollar difference is small, particularly where expenditure per person is low for both groups.

Absolute dollar differences are calculated by subtracting expenditure per person for non-Indigenous people from expenditure per person for First Nations people. A positive difference means expenditure per person was higher for First Nations people, while a negative difference means it was lower.

Ratios and differences should be considered together, as a disease group can have a large relative difference but a smaller dollar difference, or a large dollar difference even when the ratio is lower.

Across all disease groups, expenditure on hospitalisations was $5,683 per First Nations person compared with $3,508 per non-Indigenous person. This means First Nations expenditure was 1.6 times as high in relative terms, and $2,175 higher per person in absolute terms. ## Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25, Comparison of disease expenditure and disease burden

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/health-welfare-expenditure/health-system-spending-disease-injury-2024-25/contents/comparison-disease-expenditure-burden

# Health system spending on disease and injury in Australia 2024–25

## Comparison of disease expenditure and disease burden

Ill health has a substantial impact on the human cost of suffering and premature death, and the financial cost of health services.

This report along with the Australian Burden of Disease Study 2024 (AIHW 2024b) estimate the costs of different types of health conditions.

By looking at both the financial and human cost of diseases and injury, we gain a better understanding of the full impact of diseases.

#### What do we mean by ‘human cost’?

The human cost of particular diseases can be measured through burden of disease analysis. This looks at the impact of different diseases and injuries on a population and helps us to understand the impact of these conditions in terms of ‘years of healthy life lost’.

Losing healthy years can occur in two ways: through premature death (the fatal burden), or through living with the impacts of a disease (the non-fatal burden). Looking at these two measures together provides an overall understanding of the total burden, or human cost of disease and injury.

It is estimated that 5.8 million years of healthy life were lost due to Australians living with or dying prematurely from disease and injury in 2024 (AIHW 2024).

### Which disease groups have the greatest impact in terms of spending and burden?

Both burden of disease reporting and disease expenditure reporting provide two different lenses through which we can consider the impact of diseases.

Of all disease groups, cancer and other neoplasms had the greatest human cost (highest share of total disease burden measured as the share of total disability adjusted life years (DALY)) in 2024 and was also responsible for the most spending in 2024–25.

Figure 29 shows a comparison of spending on each of the Australian Burden of Disease groups in 2024–25, (along with 2018–19 and 2015–16 for historical comparisons) with the share of total disease burden for each disease group in 2024. Mental health conditions and substance use disorders ranked second highest in terms of disease burden but only fifth highest burden of disease group in terms of spending.

### Impact of including community and public health programs specifically linked to diseases on disease group ranking

If spending on health prevention through targeted community and public health programs, specifically linked to diseases, are included in the totals, the ranking for spending on disease groups changes. The biggest change is seen for the disease group, mental health conditions and substance use disorders that climbs from a ranking of fifth highest burden of disease group in terms of spending to third highest. Including health prevention related costs from community and public health programs in the main AIHW disease expenditure database is something that is being explored. The findings as shown in Figure 29, are that a significant amount of money is being spent on mental health conditions and substance use disorders that is not currently being captured in the standard disease expenditure reporting. One of the key barriers to including this expenditure in the AIHW Disease expenditure database on an ongoing basis is being able to allocate the program expenditure down to burden of disease condition level, age group and sex to align with other spending on primary health care, hospitals and referred medical services currently reported in the database.

#### Figure 29: Spending vs burden for each Australian Burden of Disease Group

Both measures, percent of total disease expenditure (%) and percent of total Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) provide insights into the impact of diseases on our society. By considering multiple sources of data, governments and service providers have better evidence to help them identify priorities and develop policies and strategies to meet the needs of those affected.

It is important to note, however, that diseases and their effects on people, society and the health system are complex. There can be many reasons why a disease may have a large human cost but low health spending – and vice versa.

In cases where a particular disease has relatively high human cost but low spending, it does not necessarily mean that health spending should be increased. Road transport injuries or smoking related diseases, for example, may be also addressed by investments and responses outside the health system, such as through education, transport, environmental health or other social services.

## Spending on cancer screening

This section presents spending on Australia’s national population-based cancer screening programs from 2013–14 to 2024–25. It includes total spending for breast, cervical and bowel cancer screening, as well as spending by age group and sex, and provides context on how these programs support early detection and prevention of cancer.

National population-based screening programs are organised, systematic and integrated processes of testing for signs of cancer or pre-cancerous conditions in targeted populations without obvious symptoms. Programs target specific populations and/or age groups where evidence shows screening to be most effective. This report covers the following 3 national population-based cancer screening programs which were in place during 2024–25, noting that the National Lung Cancer Screening Program was implemented in July 2025:

In 2024–25, $508.7 million was spent on these population-based screening programs, of which $312.3 million was for breast cancer screening and assessment, $152.3 million for cervical screening and $44.1 million for bowel cancer screening. In terms of total spending in 2024–25 for these 3 conditions (outside of screening costs), $2.1 billion was spent on treating breast cancer, compared to $1.9 billion for bowel cancer and $116 million for cervical cancer. Cancer was the disease group with the highest disease burden in Australia in 2024 accounting for 16.4% of total DALYs. In 2024, 1.3% of total disease burden was due to breast cancer, compared to 1.7% for bowel cancer and 0.1% for cervical cancer.

- BreastScreen Australia
- National Cervical Screening Program
- National Bowel Cancer Screening Program

### BreastScreen Australia

In 2024–25, just over $312.3 million was spent on BreastScreen Australia. BreastScreen Australia began in 1991. It aims to reduce illness and death from breast cancer through an organised approach to the early detection of breast cancer, using screening mammography to detect unsuspected breast cancer in women. BreastScreen also provides assessment services to inform cancer diagnosis. Early detection provides an opportunity for early treatment, which can reduce illness and death. Women aged 40 and over are eligible for free mammograms every 2 years, and women aged 50–74 are actively targeted to participate in the program (the target age group increased in 2013 from 50–69, to the current 50–74 years).

### National Cervical Screening Program

In 2024–25, $152.3 million was spent on the National Cervical Screening Program (NCSP). The NCSP began in 1991. It aims to reduce cervical cancer cases, illness and deaths in Australia. The NCSP originally targeted women aged 20–69 for a 2-yearly Papanicolaou (Pap) smear, or ‘Pap test’ to detect precancerous abnormalities of the cervix. From 1 December 2017, the NCSP changed to 5-yearly cervical screening for women aged 25–74 using a primary human papilloma virus test with partial HPV genotyping and reflex liquid-based cytology triage.

### National Bowel Cancer Screening Program

In 2024–25, just over $44.1 million was spent on the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (NBCSP). The NBCSP began in 2006. It aims to reduce the morbidity and mortality from bowel cancer by actively recruiting and screening the eligible target population, aged 45–74, for early detection or prevention of the disease. For those aged 50 to 74, the NBCSP automatically sends out free screening kits every two years. From 1 July 2024, individuals aged 45 to 49 can also request a free kit to be mailed to them.

### National Lung Cancer Screening Program

The National Lung Cancer Screening Program was implemented in July 2025. The Program uses low-dose computed tomography (low-dose CT) scans to look for lung cancer in high-risk people without any symptoms. It aims to find lung cancer early and save lives. Expenditure on The National Lung Cancer Screening Program will be included in future editions of this report.

### Methods used to estimate disease spending

The methods used for estimating disease spending is a mixture of ‘top-down’ and ‘bottom-up’ approaches. A ‘top-down’ approach is where total spending across the health system is estimated and then allocated to the relevant conditions based on the available service use data.

Although this approach produces consistency, good coverage and totals that add up to known expenditure, it is not as comprehensive for any specific disease as a detailed ‘bottom-up’ analysis, which would include the actual costs incurred for that disease. A lack of amenable data sources means that a more granular ‘bottom-up’ analysis is not possible.
