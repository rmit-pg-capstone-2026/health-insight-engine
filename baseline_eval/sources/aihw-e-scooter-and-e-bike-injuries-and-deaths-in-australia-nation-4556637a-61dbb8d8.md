# E-scooter and e-bike injuries and deaths in Australia: national trends and data limitations

## Summary

Injuries and deaths associated with electric scooters (e‑scooters) and electric bicycles (e‑bikes) have increased in Australia over recent years, particularly among children and young people (Berecki-Gisolf and Hayman 2024; Haghani 2025a, 2025b; Sharwood et al 2026, Vallmuur et al 2023). This trend coincides with the rapid growth in e‑mobility use, expanded shared scheme access, and changes to state and territory legislation.

This report provides a national picture of hospitalisations related to transport groups that include e‑scooters and e‑bikes between 2009–10 and 2024–25.

Although current hospitalisation data do not separately identify e-scooter and e-bike injuries, analysis of the broader transport groups within which e-scooters and e-bikes are classified can provide useful insights into emerging trends and populations at risk.

The introduction of ICD-10-AM placeholder codes from 1 July 2026 will improve the identification of injuries involving e-scooters, e-bikes and other e-mobility devices. This change is expected to substantially improve the accuracy, specificity and overall quality of data on e-mobility-related injuries in Australia.

### Scope of this report

This analysis uses **hospitalisation data** from the National Hospital Morbidity Database (NHMD). ICD-10-AM classifies the injury type, external cause, place of occurrence and activity associated with each hospitalisation. The classification of injury hospitalisations in the transport groups that include e-scooters and e-bikes are summarised in Figure 1.

**Emergency department presentations** are not included because national emergency department data do not include cause of injury information that is needed to identify these e-mobility transport groups.

**National mortality data** do not identify transport groups that include e-scooters and e-bikes in sufficient detail. Therefore, this report includes only in-hospital deaths recorded in the NHMD, not all deaths.

### Main findings

#### Most hospitalisations in the relevant transport groups are falls involving powered scooters

In 2024–25, falls involving powered scooters accounted for 61% of recorded personal transport hospitalisations.

Hospitalisation rates for falls involving powered scooters have tripled since 2019–20, reaching 15 per 100,000 population in 2024–25.

#### Place of occurrence and activity at the time of injury suggest increasing e-scooter use

Falls involving powered scooters increasingly occurred on streets and highways, rising from 20% in 2015–16 to 47% in 2024–25. Activity data were often unspecified, but among records with an assigned activity, leisure use was most common and there were small increases in cases recorded as sport, leisure and working for income.

Although the data cannot distinguish e-scooters from other powered scooters, the shift towards street and highway locations, together with activity patterns consistent with leisure and work-related use, suggests that recent increases are more likely to reflect e-scooter use than other pedestrian conveyances, such as mobility scooters.

#### Young males experience the highest hospitalisation rates for falls involving powered scooters

In 2024–25, boys aged 12–15 years had the highest hospitalisation rate of 54 per 100,000 population – a 15‑fold increase since 2019–20.

For those aged 12–15 years, boys were more than 4 times as likely as girls to be hospitalised for falls involving powered scooters.

#### Trends for transport groups that include e-bikes are less specific but still concerning

Hospitalisation rates rose for the transport group that includes e-bikes, with the largest increases among younger people, especially those aged 12–15 and 16–24 years.

#### The increase in severe outcomes largely reflects rising hospitalisation numbers

The number of hospitalisations involving severe outcomes has increased, largely reflecting the overall rise in hospitalisations rather than clear evidence that hospitalised cases are becoming more severe.

The number of in-hospital deaths among people hospitalised for falls involving powered scooters increased from 7 in 2015–16 to 20 in 2024–25. However, there was no clear change in the proportion of hospitalisations that resulted in an in-hospital death (0.7% in 2015–16 and 0.5% in 2024–25)

#### Jurisdictional differences align with policy settings and timing

Hospitalisation rates increased in all states and territories from around 2020–21, but the timing and size of increases differed by jurisdiction.

Rates were highest in Queensland and Western Australia, where e-scooter access was introduced earlier and public use has been relatively broad.

The ACT showed an early sharp increase after legalisation and shared scheme expansion, followed by a later decline.

Rates remained lowest in New South Wales, where private e-scooter use on public land remained restricted and access was largely limited to approved shared scheme trial areas.

#### Pedestrian injury trends may indicate under-capture of e-scooter and e-bike collisions

Recent increases in hospitalisations for pedestrians injured in other and unspecified transport accidents may also indicate that some e-scooter and e-bike-related pedestrian injuries are being captured within broader transport categories. The data presented in this report reflect injuries to the rider, not to a potential crash counterpart.

These findings highlight the need for more specific national coding to monitor e-mobility injuries and support prevention responses. New ICD-10-AM placeholder codes, effective from 1 July 2026, are expected to improve future national reporting by enabling hospitalisations involving e-scooters, e-bikes and other e-mobility devices to be identified more specifically.

# E-scooter and e-bike injuries and deaths in Australia: national trends and data limitations

## Understanding e-mobility injury trends and data limitations

The popularity of e-mobility devices – particularly e-scooters and e-bikes – has surged in recent years, with this rapid uptake outpacing the ability of current classification systems to accurately capture related injuries and deaths.

While existing systems cannot specifically identify injuries involving these devices, analysis of the broader transport groups within which e-scooters and e-bikes are classified can provide useful insights into emerging trends and populations at risk.

### Rising injury and fatality trends among young Australians using e-scooters and e-bikes

Injuries and deaths associated with e-scooters and e-bikes are rising in Australia, especially among children and young people (Berecki-Gisolf and Hayman 2024; Haghani 2025a, 2025b; Sharwood et al 2026, Vallmuur et al 2023).

These trends have led to state and territory inquiries and calls for better evidence to guide policy and safety measures (Parliament of New South Wales 2025; Parliament of Western Australia 2025; Queensland Parliament 2025).

### The use of e-scooters and e-bikes in Australia

E-mobility devices – including e-scooters and e-bikes – are changing the way people make short trips in their communities. Their popularity has grown following new regulations and the introduction of shared user schemes since late 2018. In 2022, an estimated 3.6 million Australians used an e-scooter (We Ride Australia 2023).

Across Australia, e‑scooter policy has evolved from early trials and legalisation to permanent regulatory frameworks, with recent reforms focused on safety, governance and integration into broader micromobility systems. E-scooter legislation varies by state and territory, but most require helmets, set age and speed limits, and prohibit riding with passengers, using mobile phones or riding intoxicated. As of August 2026, only New South Wales and the Northern Territory did not allow private e-scooters on public land, although New South Wales is moving towards legalisation.

In Australia, e-bikes that meet legal power and speed limits are classified as bicycles, meaning riders generally don't need registration, a licence, or insurance (NRMA 2025). E-bikes exceeding 25 km/hr can only be used legally on private property. As of 31 August 2026, Queensland required e-mobility users to be 16 years or above and hold at least a Queensland learner licence to use e-bikes and e-scooters unsupervised in public areas (McKay and Brewster 2026; Queensland Government 2026). However, exemptions apply and children between 12–17 years are still able to ride under parental supervision (Williams 2026).

E-bike design rules across Australia have changed quickly, with all jurisdictions now largely following the EN 15194 European standard, which covers motor power and speed. Some regulatory differences remain, especially around access and minimum age requirements.

For more information, see Appendix: E-scooter and e-bike legislation and regulation.

#### Challenges in identifying e-scooter and e-bike injuries in national data

Injuries and their causes (’external causes’) in Australian hospital data are classified using the *International statistical classification of diseases and related health problems, 10th revision, Australian modification* (ICD-10-AM) (IHACPA 2026). However, for the period covered in this report, ICD-10-AM does not classify e-scooters or e-bikes as distinct transport modes.

#### Why this matters

This lack of specificity within the classifications and data limits the ability to accurately quantify injury and fatality risk associated with these devices.

Emergency department (ED) data are even more limited than hospital data, as the classification used in ED care (the ICD-10-AM ED Principal Diagnosis Short List) does not include external cause codes. In Victoria, analysis of ED data relies on extraction from free-text narrative provided at triage (Berecki-Gisolf and Hayman 2024; Sharwood et al 2026). However, for privacy reasons, free-text information is not included in national ED datasets.

Queensland provides another example of timely, targeted e-mobility injury data. Streamlined reporting of electronic personal mobility device injuries led to a dashboard for health and transport stakeholders, reinforcing the need for more specific national data to support monitoring, policy and prevention (Grimmet and Droder 2025).

The AIHW has partnered with the University of New South Wales on a 5-year Medical Research Future Fund funded National Injury Surveillance for Actionable Research (NISAR-ED) initiative (UNSW 2026). This data infrastructure will use structured and unstructured ED data to generate ICD-11 external cause of injury information, enhancing the measurement of injury causes, monitoring of trends and support for prevention and health system responses.

#### What’s changing

As of 1 July 2026, placeholder codes for e-scooters, e-bikes and other e-mobility devices come into effect for ICD-10-AM Thirteenth Edition (IHACPA 2026). This will allow hospitalisations involving e-scooters to be distinguished from mobility scooters and other pedestrian conveyances, e-bikes to be distinguished from motorcycles, and other powered pedestrian conveyances such as powered skateboards, one-wheeled boards and e-boards to be distinguished from their non-powered counterparts.

ICD‑11 will allow injuries to be coded using clusters that capture transport mode, whether the event occurred in road‑traffic, the injured person’s role (for example, pedestrian), and what other type of vehicle was involved, if any (collision counterpart) (WHO 2022). While this will substantially improve the identification of injuries related to new transport modes, e-scooters and e-bikes are currently absent from the dedicated transport event descriptor list, however, as noted above this is in discussion at the WHO-FIC Committees and Reference Groups for inclusion.

ICD‑11 is scheduled for implementation in Australia in 2028/29 for mortality data and 2031 for hospitalisation data and therefore will not meet immediate policy, surveillance and reporting needs (AIHW 2026).

Despite these challenges, careful use of existing ICD‑10‑AM external cause codes in hospital data can provide useful insights into emerging trends and population groups at higher risk.

### Injuries to riders or drivers

Since 2019–20, hospitalisations from falls involving powered scooters have nearly tripled, reaching 15 per 100,000 population in 2024–25.

In contrast, hospitalisation rates due to falls from nonpowered scooters fell between 2020–21 (7.4 per 100,000) and 2024–25 (4.9 per 100,000). This likely reflects the increasing popularity of e-scooters over this time. Further details are provided in the section in Hospitalisations for the transport group that includes falls involving powered scooters.

Hospitalisation rates for drivers or riders of motor-scooters, mopeds, and motorised bicycles remained below 2 per 100,000 between 2009–10 and 2019–20 but rose to 4.0 and 4.4 per 100,000 by 2024–25 for collision and non-collision accidents, respectively. Further details are provided in the section Hospitalisations for transport groups that include e-bikes.

### Injuries to pedestrians

Although hospitalisations of pedestrians injured in collisions with powered scooters are relatively low, these cases nearly doubled between 2022–23 and 2023–24, from 0.3 to 0.5 per 100,000.

Hospitalisation rates for pedestrian collision injuries from two- or three-wheeled motor vehicles have remained largely stable over time. The 5-year average to 2024–25 was 0.4 per 100,000.

The category *Pedestrian injured in other and unspecified transport accidents* has seen an increase in hospitalisations in recent years. The rate rose from 0.9 per 100,000 in 2022–23 to 1.9 per 100,000 in 2023–24 and further to 2.4 per 100,000 in 2024–25.

This classification may be increasingly used to record pedestrian injuries resulting from collisions with e-scooters or e-bikes. This trend could be due to the lack of specificity in the ICD classification, particularly for e-bikes, where these incidents are not clearly distinguished from other transport accidents.

### Age and sex

The dramatic change in the hospitalisation rate for the group that includes falls involving powered scooters over the past 5 years is most likely to be due to the increased popularity of e-scooters. This section explores the demographic characteristics of these hospitalisations in more detail.

In most states and territories, public e-scooter use is not permitted among people under the age of 16 years. Queensland and the Australian Capital Territory are exceptions.

See Appendix: E-scooter and e-bike legislation and regulation.

Males were more likely to be hospitalised for falls involving powered scooters than females. In 2024–25, males accounted for around two-thirds (69%) of these hospitalisations.

Rates rose sharply among those aged 12–15 years between 2019–20 and 2024–25, reaching 33 hospitalisations per 100,000 population in 2024–25 (Figure 3).

The increase was largest among boys aged 12–15 years, with rates rising 15-fold from 3.6 to 54 per 100,000. Among girls, rates increased 5-fold, from 2.5 to 12 per 100,000.

Rate for other age groups increased more gradually or remained stable, staying below 20 hospitalisations per 100,000 population.

### Severity of injuries and in-hospital deaths

Analysis of metrics including same day discharge, length of stay (number of bed days) and time spent in an intensive care unit (ICU) or on continuous ventilatory support (CVS) can provide an insight into the severity of injuries.

Between 2015–16 and 2024–25, there has been an increase in the number of hospitalisations for falls involving powered scooters requiring ICU admission (16 and 73 hospitalisations, respectively) or CVS (6 and 51).

The number of in-hospital deaths for falls involving powered scooters increased from 7 in 2015–16 to 20 in 2024–25. Despite this increase, there was no clear change in the proportion of hospitalisations that resulted in an in-hospital death (0.7% in 2015–16 and 0.5% in 2024–25) (Figure 8). However, this does not include deaths that occur outside of hospitals.

For other outcomes:

- ICU admission was required for around 2% of powered scooter hospitalisations across the 10-year period.
- CVS remained low, with no clear change in the proportion of hospitalisations between 2015–16 (0.6%) and 2024–25 (1.2%).

## State and territory differences

### Falls involving powered scooters

Across Australia, e‑scooter reforms progressed from early trials and initial legalisation (2018–2019), through expansion of shared schemes and baseline rules (2020–2021), to tighter safety and enforcement settings (2022–2023). More recent reforms reflect a shift to permanent legislative frameworks and policy development, supported by inquiries and broader micromobility integration (from 2024). See Appendix: E-scooter and e-bike legislation and regulation for more information.

Hospitalisation rates related to falls involving powered scooters have increased since 2020–21 for all states and territories (Figure 15). Hospitalisation rates increased in all states and territories from around 2020–21, but the timing and size of increases varied by jurisdiction.

Key trends include:

- Queensland had the highest rate in 2024–25 (27 per 100,000 population). Queensland was an early adopter of e-scooter use and has allowed relatively broad public access, including private use in public areas.
- Western Australia also showed a steep rise after eRideables rules commenced in late 2021.
- The Australian Capital Territory recorded a sharp increase after personal e-scooters were legalised from late 2019, plus a rapid expansion of shared schemes from 2020. Rates later declined after changes to safety rules and scheme arrangements.
- Victoria’s rate increased after shared e-scooter trials began in 2021 and private e-scooter use was included in the trial from 2023 but remained lower than Queensland or Western Australia in 2024–25.
- South Australia remained low and relatively stable for most of the period, consistent with shared scheme trials operating before broader legislation in 2025.
- New South Wales recorded the lowest hospitalisation rate in 2024–25 (7.8 per 100,000 population). This may reflect ongoing restrictions on private e-scooter use on public land and access largely limited to approved shared scheme trial areas.
- Tasmania showed an increase after personal mobility device rules commenced in late 2021.
- The Northern Territory rate increased from 2020–21 despite public shared scheme access not commencing until the end of 2023. This trend should be interpreted cautiously because numbers are small.

## E-scooter and e-bike injuries and deaths in Australia: national trends and data limitations, Data sources and methods

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/injury/e-scooter-e-bike-injuries-deaths-in-australia/contents/technical-notes/data-sources-and-methods

# E-scooter and e-bike injuries and deaths in Australia: national trends and data limitations

## Data sources and methods

### Data sources

#### Hospitalisations

Hospitalisations data are sourced from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare’s (AIHW) National Hospital Morbidity Database (NHMD). This database includes data on virtually all hospital admissions in Australia, in both public and private hospitals. Data are collected for each episode of hospital care (called a hospital separation), which starts when a patient is admitted to hospital and ends when the hospital stay ends, or there is change in the type of care (for example, from acute care to rehabilitation hospital care).

Most diseases or conditions are recorded as a diagnosis following these criteria:

For more information about data contained in the NHMD refer to the AIHW Hospitals technical notes and glossary.

- Diseases are recorded as principal diagnosis when they are considered to be the primary reason for the patient being hospitalised.
- Diseases that coexist with the principal diagnosis at the time of hospitalisation, or arise during the episode of care, are recorded as additional diagnoses when they affect the management of patients in terms of requiring therapeutic treatment, diagnostic procedures, or increased nursing care and/or monitoring.

#### Population data

The population data used are estimated resident populations (ERPs) derived from the ABS Census of Population and Housing

For more information, see National, state and territory population

Population data are used for demographic analyses and as the denominator in calculating rates. All population level calculations are based on the estimated resident population (ERP) as at the midpoint of each financial year (December).

### Methods

#### Estimating index cases

Each record in the NHMD refers to a single episode of care in a hospital. Some injury incidents result in more than one episode of care and, therefore, more than one record.

To minimise the impact of overcounting where a person experienced multiple episodes of care relating to the same condition, the following criteria are applied to estimate incidents:

- Excludes records where admission mode is transfer from another hospital (1).
- Excludes records where admission mode is statistical admission (2) and care type is not acute (1, 7.1, 7.2).
- Excluding records where care involving use of rehabilitation procedures (Category Z50) appears as an additional diagnosis and care type is not acute (1, 7.1, 7.2).

#### Crude rates

Unless otherwise stated, crude rates are presented and, as such, these rates have not been adjusted to account for differences in the age structures of different populations.

The denominator for rate calculations was the estimated resident population (ERP) values as of 31 December for the given year (for example, crude rates for 2023–24 used the December 2023 population).

95% confidence intervals were used to support interpretation of selected results, particularly where analyses involved small cell counts, low proportions or small proportional changes over time or between groups. Confidence intervals help indicate the level of uncertainty around an estimate and whether apparent differences or changes are likely to reflect meaningful variation rather than random fluctuation.

## E-scooter and e-bike injuries and deaths in Australia: national trends and data limitations, Identification of e-scooter and e-bike related hospitalisations

Source: https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/injury/e-scooter-e-bike-injuries-deaths-in-australia/contents/technical-notes/identification-e-scooter-e-bike-hospitalisations

# E-scooter and e-bike injuries and deaths in Australia: national trends and data limitations

## Identification of e-scooter and e-bike related hospitalisations

This report used ICD-10-AM external cause codes that classify e-scooter and e-bike injuries that require hospitalisation.

ICD-10-AM external cause codes used for classification of injuries related to e-scooters and e-bikes are summarised in Table 2. Groupings used for the analyses presented in this report are also indicated.

E-scooters: ICD-10-AM codes (for hospitalisations) for e-scooter injuries are classified as ‘powered scooters’ which also include mobility scooters (that is, gophers) and motorised low-powered scooters used by children. In the case of injury to the rider, falls involving powered scooters are in a category for *other and unspecified pedestrian* conveyance which also includes falls from a shopping trolley.

E-bikes: E-bikes are classified as motorcycles in the ICD-10-AM national coding rules for hospitalisations (IHACPA 2026). However, legal e-bikes are generally treated as bicycles in each state and territory, requiring no registration, license or insurance (NRMA 2025).

ICD-10-AM codes for e-bikes are less granular than for e-scooters and include mopeds (small motorcycles) and motor scooters. Injuries to riders or passengers are coded in category V20-V29 *Motorcycle rider injured in transport accident* with a 5<sup>th</sup> character ‘0’ *motor scooter, moped or motorised bicycle*. There is no specific coding advice for injuries to pedestrians from e-bikes. Pedestrians hit by e-bikes are coded under category V02 *Pedestrian injured in collision with two- or three-wheeled motor vehicle*. This would include motorcycles and therefore is not specific enough to gain insight into e-bike injuries to pedestrians.

Other codes: for comparison, injuries involving falls from non-powered scooters (code W02.6) and pedestrians injured in other and unspecified transport accidents (category V09) were also identified.

### Timeline of e-scooter reform

Across Australia, e‑scooter policy reform has progressed in distinct but overlapping waves (Table A2).

#### Wave 1: Early trials and first legalisation (2018–2019)

The first reform wave focused on enabling experimentation and early uptake. Queensland moved earliest, introducing shared e‑scooters in late 2018 alongside legalisation for personal use (Caldwell 2018; Queensland Government 2018). South Australia followed with trial-based approaches involving local councils, while the ACT legalised personal e‑scooters in 2019 (ACT Greens 2019; Government of South Australia 2023). This period was characterised by pilots and initial permissions, rather than comprehensive regulatory frameworks.

#### Wave 2: Expansion of shared schemes and basic frameworks (2020–2021)

From 2020, jurisdictions began to scale up shared e‑scooter schemes and introduce foundational rules. The ACT rolled out shared schemes and strengthened safety rules, while Tasmania and Western Australia introduced formal personal mobility device (PMD) or eRideables frameworks in 2021 (ACT Government 2021; Gianinni 2020; Tasmanian Government 2021; WA Government 2021). Victoria and New South Wales also commenced structured trials during this period (City of Ballarat 2021; New South Wales Government 2022). This wave marked a shift from isolated trials to statewide or territory-wide operating rules

#### Wave 3: Rule tightening and integration into road law (2022–2023)

As usage increased, reform shifted towards clarifying and tightening rules. Queensland undertook major PMD rule reform in 2022, aligning speed limits and penalties with broader road rules (Courty 2022). Victoria extended and expanded trials in 2023, alongside legalising privately owned e‑scooters (Victoria Walks 2023). The Northern Territory joined later, introducing shared e‑scooters in Darwin in 2023 (City of Darwin 2023). Overall, this phase focused on safety, enforcement and consistency.

#### Wave 4: Transition from trials to permanent arrangements (2024–2025)

Between 2024 and 2025, reforms moved decisively toward permanent legislative frameworks. Victoria concluded trials and implemented permanent statewide rules with tougher penalties (Allen 2024). South Australia passed enabling legislation in 2024, followed by full legalisation in 2025 (Government of South Australia 2025). New South Wales advanced through an action plan, parliamentary inquiry, and public signalling of intent to legalise (Parliament of New South Wales 2025). Western Australia undertook a parliamentary inquiry, framing a future reform agenda (Parliament of Western Australia 2025). This wave reflects policy consolidation and formalisation.

#### Wave 5: Comprehensive reviews and next-generation reforms (2025–2026)

The most recent wave centres on system-wide reviews and reform refinement. Queensland and Western Australia completed inquiries and published reform reports or government responses, with Queensland introducing and commencing new legislation in 2026 (Parliament of Western Australia 2025; Queensland Parliament 2026; ten Broeke 2026; Williams 2026). The ACT expanded shared schemes to include shared e‑bikes, and Tasmania decided to remove shared e-scooters in favour of shared e-bikes in Hobart (ACT Government 2026; Steel 2026; ten Broeke 2026). These reforms increasingly focus on risk management, age and licensing requirements, scheme governance, and integration with broader micromobility policy.

### Timeline of e-bike reform

E-bikes have been in use in Australia for more than a decade.

From 30 May 2012, the Australian Design Rules were amended to recognise Electric Power Assisted Bicycles (EPAC) that meet the European standard EN15194 as a distinct category of bicycle (Australian Government 2012).

Key aspects of EN15194 are:

Over the following 5 years all states and territories adopted EN15194, with NSW being the last in 2017 (We Ride Australia 2025). Some jurisdictions also recognise ≤200 W power-assisted pedal cycles as a separate category; however, these are not commonly available in Australian retailers any longer (Transport Victoria n.d.)

Key regulatory shifts include:

In January 2021, the Federal Government amended the Motor Vehicle Standards Act, removing mandatory international safety standards (EN15194) for imported e-bikes (Australian Government 2021). This change effectively deregulated the import process, which allowed large numbers of high-powered, non-compliant and unsafe e-bikes (including battery hazards) to enter Australia (We Ride Australia 2025; Queensland Parliament 2026).

In February 2023, New South Wales departed from EN15194 by permitting e‑bikes with a maximum continuous rated power of up to 500 W, exceeding the 250 W limit specified under the EN standard (NSW Government 2023).

In December 2025, the Federal Government updated import laws requiring that e-bikes imported into Australia must now meet EN15194:2017 (Australian Government 2025).

In March 2026, NSW returned to the ≤250 W standard with a 3-year transition period away from higher powered e-bikes to EN15195 between 2026 and 2029 and is reviewing age limits (NSW Government n.d; Parliament of New South Wales 2026, Roberts 2026).

With NSW reverting to the national standard in 2026, all jurisdictions will broadly align with the EN15194 / EPAC definition (250 W pedal‑assist, 25 km/h cut‑off), with remaining differences around access and age rules.

- Pedal‑assist (pedelec): the electric motor must only provide assistance when the rider is pedalling, requiring a pedalling mechanism to operate.
- Power limitation: the motor’s maximum continuous rated power is limited to 250 W.
- Speed cut-off: motor assistance must cut off automatically when the bike reaches 25 km/h, or earlier if the rider stops pedalling (CEN 2011).

- A phase of relaxed import controls between 2021 and 2025.

- A temporary period in New South Wales between 2023 and 2025 allowing higher-powered e-bikes.

- A nationwide move to EN15194 from 2026.
