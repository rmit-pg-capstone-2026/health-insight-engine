# B. Candidate A cross-case profile

### Deterministic results

Candidate A performed very consistently in Case 1, with scores of **93.33, 93.33 and 96.67**, no Hard Gate FAILs, and a **3.34-point range**. In Case 2, the scores became **93.3, 88.3 and 60.0**, producing a much larger **33.3-point range** and one HG-4 FAIL in A3. Its case mean consequently moved from **94.44 in Case 1 to 80.53 in Case 2**, a difference of **13.91 points**.  

Relevance was completely stable: all six Candidate A executions across the two cases returned **Relevant**, and QD-3 remained **3.00 in both cases**.

Priority was stable within each case but not across cases. Candidate A returned **Medium Priority ×3** on Dementia and **Informational ×3** on the ABS case. Correspondingly, QD-4 moved from **3.00 to 1.67**.

Evidence reliability was strong overall in the sense that **HG-2 passed in all six runs**. However, Case 2 A3 contained the recorded material `comparison_period` error under HG-4 and also had weaker finding completeness and caveat handling. In Case 1, all three runs instead shared the less severe qualification omission concerning the estimated/modelled status of the DALY figure.  

### Cross-case interpretation

Candidate A's clearest replicated strength is **relevance reasoning and evidence-boundary discipline**. Even when A3 deteriorated sharply in Case 2, relevance remained correct and QD-3 stayed at the maximum recorded score.

Its principal cross-case concern is **robustness rather than a single universally repeated factual error**. Case 1 showed strong, narrow score variation; Case 2 exposed a large downside run in which finding selection, temporal fidelity, priority reasoning, uncertainty handling, follow-up usefulness and clarity all declined.

The priority behaviour also appears more case-sensitive than Candidate B's. Candidate A changed from Medium to Informational between the two cases while remaining perfectly consistent within each individual case.

# C. Candidate B cross-case profile

### Deterministic results

Candidate B scored **93.33 in all three Case 1 runs** and **86.7 in all three Case 2 runs**. Its run-score range was therefore **0.00 in both cases**. The two case means differ by **6.63 points**, but there was no within-case score variation at all.  

Across both cases, all six outputs were **Relevant** and all six were **Medium Priority**. QD-3 stayed at **3.00**, while QD-1, QD-2 and QD-6 were also **3.00 in both cases**.

Candidate B recorded **no Hard Gate FAIL across either case**, but every run had an HG-4 REVIEW. The underlying reason changed by case:

* in Case 1, the recurring issue was failure to state explicitly that the DALY burden estimate was estimated/modelled;
* in Case 2, the recurring issue was failure to surface the unresolved 30 June versus 31 July cutoff inconsistency.

HG-2 nevertheless passed in all six Candidate B executions, and QD-2 remained **3.00 in both cases**.  

### Cross-case interpretation

Candidate B's strongest replicated property is **reproducibility**. The same major strengths—finding selection, grounding, relevance reasoning and follow-up usefulness—were retained across both very different cases.

Its most evident recurring weakness is **qualification/caveat preservation**. The exact omitted qualification differs between the two cases, so it is not the same factual mistake repeated across sources. However, the broader behavioural pattern is the same: a material or potentially material source qualification was omitted often enough to produce **HG-4 REVIEW in all six runs and QD-5 = 2.00 in both cases**.

QD-7 also remained **2.00 in both cases**, making output efficiency/conciseness another replicated, though less severe, limitation.

# D. Replicated findings across cases

The following findings reproduce across both evaluation cases:

1. **Both candidates are highly reliable on relevance.** Each produced `Relevant` in all six runs across the two cases, with QD-3 = **3.00 in every case-level average**.

2. **Both candidates maintained strong evidence grounding overall.** HG-2 passed in all twelve executions across both candidates. Candidate B maintained QD-2 = **3.00** in both cases; Candidate A was 3.00 in Case 1 and 2.67 in Case 2.

3. **Candidate B is consistently more reproducible at the run-score level.** Its score range was **0.00 in both cases**. Candidate A's range was 3.34 in Case 1 but expanded to 33.3 in Case 2.

4. **Candidate B has a recurring caveat-preservation issue.** QD-5 remained **2.00 in both cases**, and HG-4 was REVIEW in all six B runs, although the specific missing qualification differed by case.

5. **Candidate B's finding selection and follow-up usefulness were highly stable.** QD-1 and QD-6 remained **3.00 in both cases**.

6. **Candidate A's strongest stable dimension is relevance reasoning.** QD-3 remained 3.00 despite substantial Case 2 variation elsewhere.

7. **Both candidate families showed weaker priority calibration in Case 2 than Case 1.** QD-4 moved from 3.00 to 1.67 for A and from 3.00 to 2.00 for B. This indicates that the ABS case discriminated the prompt families more strongly on prioritisation than the Dementia case did.

# E. Case-specific findings

### Case 1 — AIHW Dementia

The two candidates were extremely similar. Both had no FAILs, identical relevance and priority labels, identical QD-1 through QD-6 averages, and only a small QD-7 difference. Candidate A's **1.11-point higher mean** came entirely from A3 receiving the higher clarity/efficiency score.

The missing estimated/modelled qualification for the DALY result was a **shared case-specific issue across all six outputs**. There was no evidence in Case 1 of Candidate A's later large run-to-run instability. 

### Case 2 — ABS Acute Respiratory

The candidate profiles separated much more clearly.

Candidate A produced two strong all-PASS runs but one materially weaker run with **HG-4 FAIL** and a 60.0 score. Its finding-selection completeness and priority calibration also varied substantially.

Candidate B reproduced exactly the same score and dimension profile three times but reproduced the same HG-4 REVIEW issue as well.

Priority also became strongly candidate-dependent: A produced **Informational ×3**, whereas B produced **Medium Priority ×3**. Thus, unlike the Dementia case where both candidate families converged on Medium, the ABS case exposed a systematic difference in their priority behaviour.

# F. Cross-case stability and reliability comparison

### Deterministic comparison

Across the recorded measures, **Candidate B exhibits the more stable cross-case behaviour**.

Its within-case score range was **0 in both cases**, relevance and priority labels remained unchanged across all six executions, and QD-1, QD-2, QD-3 and QD-6 remained exactly 3.00 across both cases.

Candidate A was stable in Case 1 but not Case 2. Its case mean changed by **13.91 points**, versus **6.63 points** for B, and its run-score range changed from 3.34 to 33.3. Several dimension means also moved materially between the cases.

Hard Gate reliability requires a more nuanced interpretation. Across six runs:

* **Candidate A:** 2 fully PASS runs, 3 REVIEW runs, 1 FAIL run.
* **Candidate B:** 0 fully PASS runs, 6 REVIEW runs, 0 FAIL runs.

At the individual-gate level:

* **Candidate A:** 32 PASS, 3 REVIEW, 1 FAIL.
* **Candidate B:** 30 PASS, 6 REVIEW, 0 FAIL.

So Candidate A demonstrated that it can produce completely clean runs, but it also produced the only Hard Gate FAIL. Candidate B never failed, but its REVIEW-level caveat issue was persistent rather than occasional.  

### Cross-case interpretation

The two candidates therefore show different reliability profiles.

**Candidate A's risk is downside variability:** when it performs well it can produce fully clean results, but Case 2 shows that one run can deteriorate substantially.

**Candidate B's risk is systematic imperfection:** its outputs are highly reproducible, but the same class of caveat-fidelity problem persists across cases.

Neither pattern can be reduced simply to "more PASSes" or "higher mean score." They represent different failure modes that would matter differently in deployment.

# G. Evidence still needed before final candidate selection

The supplied reports do **not** establish a frozen rule under which two completed cases are sufficient to select the final Prompt v1 candidate. Case 1 explicitly states that the Dementia development case alone was insufficient for a final decision, while the Case 2 report also stops at a case-specific comparison rather than declaring a winner.  

The cross-case evidence now establishes several useful patterns, but it also reveals genuine case dependence: Candidate A was highly stable in Dementia but much less stable in the ABS case; both candidates' priority calibration weakened in Case 2; and Candidate B's caveat-handling weakness replicated despite the specific caveat changing.

Accordingly, **another held-out case is still warranted before final candidate selection**. The most informative additional case would be one that can test the behaviours that remain unresolved by the first two cases: whether Candidate A's Case 2 failure was isolated or recurring, whether Candidate B continues to omit important qualifications, and whether the A/B priority divergence persists on another evidence structure or borderline prioritisation case.

The current two-case evidence supports a cross-case characterisation of both candidates, but it does not yet supply a sufficiently robust basis—or a frozen protocol rule—for making the final candidate decision.
